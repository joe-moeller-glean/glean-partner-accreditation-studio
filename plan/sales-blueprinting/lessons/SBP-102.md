# SBP-102 Sales Stage Integration

## SBP-102-L01 Stage 2 entry and qualification
@src S11 S12 S21

A Blueprint is an investment of the customer's time and the account team's time. Run one only when there is a qualified business problem worth mapping. This lesson covers where the Blueprint fits in the sales motion and what must be true before you start.

### Where the Blueprint sits
@src S11
@quote S11 | S2 Blueprint (Use Case Mapping) S3 Executive Alignment S4 Technical & Security Alignment S5 Validation Event S6 Business Value Justification S7 Procurement
@quote S11 | Stage 2 begins after a successful NBM confirms the business problem, champion path, and commitment to a formal evaluation.
In Glean's documented new-business motion (February 2026 enablement view), the Blueprint has its own stage. Stage 2 is **Blueprint (Use Case Mapping)**, where Visioning and Day-in-the-Life work happen. It is followed by Executive Alignment, Technical and Security Alignment, a Validation Event, Business Value Justification, and Procurement.

Stage 2 begins after a successful New Business Meeting (NBM). That meeting confirms the business problem, a path to a champion, and the customer's commitment to a formal evaluation. Stage labels and required fields are internal and change over time. Learn the business gates, not CRM screens.

### What must be true before you start
- **A business priority.** A named problem connected to something leadership already cares about, ideally in the customer's own words.
- **Stakeholder access.** A champion who can bring business owners and front-line users into the room, and a credible path to the executive sponsor.
- **A value hypothesis.** An early, testable statement of where value might come from.
- **The three whys.** Why change? Why now? Why Glean? Partial answers are fine at this point. Having no answers is a signal to keep qualifying.

### A trial request is not a qualified Blueprint
Many evaluations begin with "Can we just try it?" A free trial without a business owner, success measure, or sponsor is the "pilot purgatory" the Blueprint exists to prevent. The answer is not to rename the trial as a Blueprint. Do more qualification first, then offer the Blueprint as the faster route to a decision.

> **Watch for:** An enthusiastic contact with no access to business owners is not the same as an aligned champion. Establish the business problem and a path to sponsorship before you book broad discovery sessions.

### Handle "just show me the product"
@src S68
Glean's onboarding training addresses a common Stage 2 objection: "Why do workshops when I haven't even seen the product?" The suggested response is to give a short, relevant look at the product, then explain that a tailored demo built on the customer's own priorities will be far more useful. Getting there requires understanding those priorities with their team, which is exactly what Visioning and DITL do.

Use the objection as a bridge into the Blueprint, not a reason to skip it. The goal is not to stall the deal but to earn the evidence for a better next step.

Figure: flow | Stage 2 entry check | A four-step entry check before starting the Blueprint: confirm a business priority, confirm stakeholder access, draft a value hypothesis, and answer the three whys. Only then does the Blueprint begin.
- Business priority :: A real problem a leader owns
- Stakeholder access :: Business owners and users will take part
- Value hypothesis :: A first, labeled guess at the outcome
- *Three whys :: Why change, why now, why Glean

### Practice
A contact at a fictional mid-size insurer says, "We don't need workshops. Just give us a free trial for the whole company." Decide whether to launch a Blueprint, and write the two or three questions you would ask next.

### Worked response
Not yet. This is a qualification gap, not a Blueprint opportunity. Next questions:
- "What would need to change for this to be worth the effort, and who owns that result?" (Why change, and the business owner.)
- "Why is this a priority this quarter rather than next year?" (Why now.)
- "Who would make the investment decision, and what would they need to see?" (The sponsor path and decision criteria.)

Once a priority, an owner, and a sponsor path are established, offer the Blueprint as the way to reach a decision faster than an open-ended trial.

## SBP-102-L02 Stage 2 outputs and exit evidence
@src S04 S11 S12

Stage progress should reflect evidence, not a completed calendar event. "We held the Visioning workshop" is an activity. "We have three prioritized use cases with named owners and draft success measures" is evidence.

### What Stage 2 should produce
@src S11 S04
@quote S11 | Use case workshops + visioning to lock scope, define Positive Business Outcomes (PBOs), and align on timeline and decision criteria.
Glean's stage guidance describes the purpose of Stage 2 as use case workshops and visioning that lock scope, define Positive Business Outcomes (PBOs), and align on timeline and decision criteria. By the end of the stage, the account team should be able to show:
- A champion, and the department and primary use cases in scope.
- The problem in the customer's own words, plus the three whys.
- Required capabilities: what must be true for the solution to succeed.
- The future state: how the work changes and which outcomes it supports.
- Known competition and alternatives.
- A mutual action plan (MAP) that the customer has seen and accepted.

### An evidence pack, not a checkbox
A useful Stage 2 evidence pack links the artifacts the Blueprint produces:

Table: Stage 2 evidence pack
| Evidence | Produced in | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| Use case log and impact/effort matrix | Visioning | Priorities were agreed, not assumed |
| Named business owner per use case | Visioning and DITL | Someone will own the outcome |
| Current-state and future-state workflow maps | DITL | The work and its friction were observed |
| Metric definitions and draft baselines | DITL | Value can be measured |
| Data and connector dependencies | DITL | Feasibility questions are known |
| Scheduled executive decision meeting | End of Stage 2 | There is a path to a decision |

The account team owns the exact required CRM fields. Partners contribute evidence; they are not tested on internal systems.

> **Boundary:** Don't mark a stage complete just because a workshop happened. If priorities or stakeholders are still unclear, assign focused follow-up before moving on.

### Run the right number of sessions
@src S68 S75
Glean's training is explicit that Stage 2 isn't one size fits all. For a corporate account, Visioning may be a single 45- to 60-minute session. Commercial, enterprise, and strategic accounts may need several sessions, sometimes one per department head, before the priorities are clear. A strategic, wall-to-wall evaluation usually runs several DITL interviews as well.

Whatever the number of sessions, the exit evidence is the same: agreed priorities, owners, workflow maps, draft metrics, known dependencies, and a booked executive decision.

Figure: cards | The Stage 2 evidence pack | Six cards listing the evidence Stage 2 should leave behind: a use case log and matrix, a named owner per use case, current and future workflow maps, metric definitions and draft baselines, data and connector dependencies, and a scheduled executive decision meeting.
- Use case log and matrix :: Priorities agreed, not assumed
- Named owners :: Someone accountable for each use case
- Workflow maps :: Current and future state from interviews
- Metrics and baselines :: How value will be measured
- Dependencies :: Data and connector questions known early
- *Executive meeting booked :: A path to a real decision

### Practice
An opportunity note says "Visioning done." There are no use case owners recorded. List the missing evidence and the smallest follow-up that would close the gap.

### Worked response
Missing: agreed priorities, owner names, metric definitions, workflow and data validation, and a sponsor path.

The smallest follow-up is targeted, not a repeat of Visioning. Hold a short session with the champion to confirm the top two or three use cases and name an owner for each, then book DITL interviews with those owners. Record what remains unknown, such as baselines and data access, as actions with owners and dates.

## SBP-102-L03 Stage 3 Executive Validation
@src S14 S12 S02 S11

Stage 3 is where leadership decides whether the value case justifies further investment and how it will be proven. It is not a replay of the product overview.

### What the executive conversation does
@src S11 S14 S02
@quote S11 | Engage executive sponsors and the economic buyer; align on the commercial path.
@quote S14 | Confirm success criteria and Executive Sponsorship
In the documented sales motion, Stage 3 is Executive Alignment. It engages the executive sponsor and the economic buyer and aligns on the commercial path. The joint AE and SE conversation connects the prioritized workflows and their value to leadership's objectives. It then asks for four commitments:
1. **Sponsorship.** The executive will back the effort and its resourcing.
2. **Success criteria.** The measures that define a successful outcome.
3. **The validation path.** How value and feasibility will be proven, using the lightest sufficient option.
4. **The decision process.** Who decides, what happens next, and when.

### Evidence the stage should leave behind
@src S11 S14
By the time Stage 3 closes, the record should show the economic buyer and executive sponsor, metrics tied to Positive Business Outcomes, the decision process (steps and influencers), and the decision criteria (formal and informal). Capture the sponsor's commitments in the opportunity record and the mutual action plan.

The sponsor should be able to say in their own words what business change matters, how progress will be judged, and what happens after the validation event.

### Champion approval is not executive alignment
A champion's enthusiasm is valuable, but it is not a decision. If the decision-maker can't attend, call the meeting what it is: a preparatory review. Don't record it as executive validation. Reschedule the decision with the executive rather than proceeding on a proxy's approval.

### Who counts as the executive
@src S74
@quote S74 | someone with some form of power above our champion can say, yes
Glean's Executive Readout training acknowledges that a C-suite sponsor isn't always available, especially in large accounts. What matters is that someone with power above the champion confirms the priority, ideally a person who holds the budget or can shift it. The champion's enthusiasm alone doesn't meet the bar.

The same training places the readout after DITL and before any pilot or evaluation is agreed. That is the point where the executive confirms what will be validated and why.

Figure: cards | What Executive Validation must confirm | Four cards listing what the executive conversation must confirm: sponsorship, success criteria, the validation path, and the decision process.
- Sponsorship :: Someone above the champion backs the effort
- Success criteria :: Measurable, with owners and dates
- *Validation path :: The lightest event that answers the open question
- Decision process :: Who decides, how, and when

### Practice
Write the Stage 3 exit evidence for a fictional legal department evaluation. Use customer-neutral language that you could paste into a recap.

### Worked response
"Executive sponsorship confirmed by the General Counsel. The business case and its assumptions were reviewed and accepted as directional. Success criteria agreed: contract-review cycle time and the rate of escalations to outside counsel, each with a baseline plan. Validation path agreed: a tailored demo on representative, non-confidential documents, followed by a decision meeting. Owners, decision process, and timeline are recorded in the mutual action plan."

Uncertainty to name: the baseline for outside-counsel escalations depends on a data export that the legal operations lead has not yet confirmed.

## SBP-102-L04 MEDDPICCS evidence from Blueprint
@src S17 S12 S46

Glean account teams qualify opportunities with MEDDPICCS. Blueprint work produces evidence for most of its elements. It supports the account team's judgment; it does not replace it.

### The elements and where Blueprint evidence comes from
@src S17
@quote S17 | Quantifiable measures that indicate the success of the solution and the business.
@quote S17 | The Decision Criteria is what the decision will be based on and the Decision Process is how the decision will be made.
Table: MEDDPICCS and Blueprint evidence
| Element | What it means | Blueprint evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Metrics | Quantifiable measures of success for the solution and the business | Metric cards from DITL: definition, baseline plan, target, data source, owner |
| Economic Buyer | The person with authority to approve the purchase and control the budget | Executive Validation attendance and commitments |
| Decision Criteria | What the decision will be based on: technical, economic, and relationship factors | Required capabilities and success criteria agreed with the sponsor |
| Decision Process | How the decision will be made: steps, approvers, timeline | Mutual action plan milestones and named approvers |
| Paper Process | The formal path to signed contracts | Procurement and legal checkpoints in the MAP |
| Implicate Pain | The problem, its business impact, and the cost of inaction | Current-state DITL maps and the problem in the customer's words |
| Champion | An insider who sells for you when you're not in the room | Who convened the sessions and secured access |
| Competition | Alternatives, including doing nothing or building in-house | Tools and workarounds observed during DITL |
| Success Plan | How the outcome will be delivered and measured after the decision | The validation plan linked to implementation and measurement |

### Three kinds of metrics
@src S17
Glean's MEDDPICCS material separates metrics into three kinds. **M1** metrics are outcomes delivered for existing customers. **M2** metrics are personalized to this customer. **M3** metrics are M2s validated after go-live. Blueprint work mostly produces M2s. Never present an M1 from another customer as this customer's expected result.

> **Boundary:** A filled field is not proof. Label unconfirmed information, link the supporting notes or customer artifacts, and never invent an economic buyer or a buying deadline.

### Why the champion test matters
@src S65
@quote S65 | Am I giving them a strong enough story to go sell this internally?
Glean's value-selling training makes a blunt point: assume Glean is not a budgeted initiative. Funding usually depends on a real champion, someone with political capital, carrying a clear value story into rooms you're not in. The story has to justify spending that "wasn't in the plan." The self-check the training recommends is whether you've given the champion a story strong enough to sell internally without you.

Blueprint evidence gives the champion that story: the customer's own priorities, workflows, and numbers.

Figure: cards | Where Blueprint evidence feeds qualification | Six cards linking Blueprint evidence to qualification elements: metrics from DITL and metric cards, the economic buyer from Executive Validation, decision criteria from success criteria, implicate pain from the cost of inaction, the champion from who carries the story, and the success plan from the handoff.
- Metrics :: Metric cards and DITL baselines
- Economic Buyer :: Confirmed in Executive Validation
- Decision Criteria :: Agreed success criteria
- Implicate Pain :: The cost of inaction from DITL
- *Champion :: Carries the value story internally
- Success Plan :: The handoff and measurement plan

### Practice
Map three facts to MEDDPICCS elements: the support operations director owns handle time and has a baseline report; the CFO attended the executive readout; procurement needs a security questionnaire before signature.

### Worked response
- **Director with handle-time baseline:** supports Metrics. It may also indicate a Champion, but that needs confirming.
- **CFO at the readout:** supports Economic Buyer, if the CFO controls the budget, and the Decision Process.
- **Security questionnaire before signature:** supports Paper Process and Decision Process, and becomes a MAP milestone with an owner.

Each needs linked evidence, and each stays labeled "unconfirmed" until the customer confirms it.

## SBP-102-L05 Recovering incomplete stage evidence
@src S12 S14 S13

Blueprints rarely go perfectly. A workshop may be strong while a sponsor is missing, or a use case may be compelling with no one able to supply baseline data. The skill is choosing the smallest corrective action that closes the gap.

### Match the fix to the gap
Table: Corrective actions by gap
| Gap | Smallest corrective action | Typical owner |
|---|---|---|
| No agreed priorities | Short prioritization session with the champion | Partner or SE facilitates |
| No business owner for a use case | Champion introduces the owner; quick confirmation call | AE, with the champion |
| No baseline or metric owner | Measurement-design session with the system owner, with a due date | SE or partner, with the customer data owner |
| Workflow assumptions unvalidated | Workflow playback with the users interviewed | Partner or SE |
| No executive sponsor path | Sponsor-alignment meeting; requalify if none emerges | AE |
| Value hypothesis no longer fits | Revise the hypothesis before modeling more value | SE, with the business owner |

### Progress means a better-informed decision
@src S13
@quote S13 | If you don't know the "before," the "after" is meaningless.
Progress is not more meetings. It is the next decision becoming better informed. If no executive priority supports a use case, go back to qualification before building a bigger savings model. Without a baseline, any claimed improvement cannot be verified, so a missing baseline is an action to resolve, not a number to estimate quietly.

> **Boundary:** Don't quietly lower success criteria or backfill invented numbers to move the stage forward. Record the gap, its owner, the corrective action, and the decision checkpoint.

### At a glance
Most incomplete stages have a small, specific fix. Name the gap, then take the smallest step that closes it.

Figure: compare | Match the fix to the gap | A comparison listing common gaps in stage evidence with the smallest corrective action for each, such as a prioritization session for missing priorities and a sponsor-alignment meeting for a missing sponsor path.
- Gap || Smallest corrective action
- No agreed priorities || Short prioritization session with the champion
- No owner for a use case || Champion introduces the owner
- No baseline or metric owner || Measurement-design session with the system owner
- Unvalidated workflow || Playback with the users who do the work
- *No sponsor path || Sponsor-alignment meeting, or requalify

### Practice
A Visioning session went very well, but there is no executive sponsor and no one owns the key metric. Write a short recovery plan with owners.

### Worked response
- **AE:** secures a sponsor conversation through the champion and requalifies the opportunity if no sponsor emerges by the agreed date.
- **SE or partner:** identifies the measurement dependency, meaning which system holds the metric and who can export a baseline.
- **Customer champion:** names the metric owner and books a 30-minute measurement-design session.

Hold executive validation until there is decision-ready evidence. Record each gap and its due date in the mutual action plan.

## SBP-102-L06 From executive decision to technical alignment
@src S11 S14 S16

After the executive accepts the value case, the work splits into parallel streams. Keep them linked but distinct, so that one stream's success isn't mistaken for another's.

### Three streams after the value decision
@src S11 S16
@quote S11 | Complete security/IT reviews and evaluation planning so there are no late‑stage surprises.
In the documented sales motion, Technical and Security Alignment follows Executive Alignment. Its purpose is to complete security and IT reviews and plan the evaluation so there are no late surprises. In parallel, the account team manages the commercial path.
- **Feasibility and validation.** Answer specific, unresolved buying questions with the lightest sufficient option. Glean's technical validation options range from a custom tailored demo, which needs little customer preparation, through a hands-on sandbox to a pilot or proof of concept, which needs much more preparation and time.
- **Security and IT review.** Follow the customer's approved review path, with named owners on both sides.
- **Commercial and paper process.** Signature authority, procurement steps, and legal review stay with the AE and the customer's procurement team.

### What a pilot requires
@src S16
@quote S16 | Technical Use cases and test plan documented and mutually agreed upon
If a pilot is truly needed, Glean's guidance expects groundwork first: the three whys, an identified economic buyer who has confirmed the need in an executive alignment meeting, agreement on the value hypothesis and pilot goals, documented technical use cases and a test plan, and measurable success criteria agreed with the customer. Pilots also go through Glean's internal review and approval. Partners should not promise one.

> **Boundary:** Value alignment is not technical sign-off, and a successful demo is not procurement approval. Do not promise product capabilities or support based on old decks; confirm current behavior with the Glean account team.

### Why pilots convert, and what that implies
@src S67
Glean's validation-events training offers a useful explanation of why pilots usually convert. By the time a pilot can start, almost everything a closed deal needs has already happened: a security review, the Blueprint, an economic buyer who has said what matters, executive buy-in, pricing and budget conversations, and IT ready to set up connectors.

The lesson isn't that pilots are magic. It's that the work in these three streams is what makes a deal real. A pilot started without them is a test with no path to a decision.

Figure: compare | Three streams after the value decision | A comparison of the three parallel streams after Executive Validation, with their typical owners: feasibility and validation, owned by the SE with the customer's technical owner; security and IT review, owned by the customer's security team with Glean support; and the commercial and paper process, owned by the AE with procurement.
- Stream || What it resolves
- Feasibility and validation || Can the priority workflows work on this customer's systems?
- Security and IT review || Is data handling acceptable to the customer?
- *Commercial and paper process || Budget, terms, and the path to signature

### Practice
After Executive Validation, three issues remain: whether a critical workflow is feasible, a pending security review, and unclear signature authority. Assign each to a stream and an owner.

### Worked response
- **Workflow feasibility:** a scoped validation, such as a tailored demo on representative data with agreed test cases. The SE owns it.
- **Security review:** the customer's approved review path, with a named security contact and a Glean counterpart.
- **Signature authority and procurement sequencing:** the AE and customer procurement, as MAP milestones with dates.

Each is a separate workstream with a named owner, dependencies, and a decision date.
