# SBP-109 Executive Validation and Decision

## SBP-109-L01 Executive Validation readiness
@src S14 S04 S02

Executive Validation, also called the Executive Readout, is the last step of the Blueprint Consultation. It is where leadership decides whether the value justifies investing more time and resources, and how it will be proven.

### Why this meeting matters
@src S02
@quote S02 | It is a mandatory step in moving forward with a Pilot or Proof of Value and ensuring that there is alignment with our team and client leadership before we invest more time and resources into the evaluation of Glean.
Glean's Blueprint playbook describes the Executive Readout as a mandatory step before moving forward with a pilot or proof of value. It ensures alignment between the Glean team and the customer's leadership before more time and resources go into the evaluation.

### The preflight, one to three business days before
@src S14
@quote S14 | Pre‑Work (complete 1–3 business days before)
Glean's Executive Validation checklist asks the team to confirm, one to three business days ahead:
- **Prerequisites:** economic buyer alignment, a value hypothesis, and Positive Business Outcomes tied to near-term priorities.
- **Current and future workflows** documented for the prioritized use cases.
- **An initial value estimate** built with the ROI calculator.
- **A tailored demo or Glean Lab scenario** that illustrates the future state.
- **Meeting objectives and success criteria**, so there is a clear go or no-go for sponsorship.
- **Attendees confirmed:** the executive sponsor, the technical champion, and the AE and SE owners.
- **Logistics:** a 60-minute session with the agenda circulated.
- **The decision process and timeline**, including any cloud partner.

### Ask four preflight questions
1. Which decision do we need?
2. What evidence supports it?
3. Who can make it?
4. What happens next if the answer is yes, and if it is no?

Share the gaps early enough to resolve them. Don't surprise the sponsor.

> **Boundary:** An unfinished ROI hypothesis is acceptable if it is framed transparently. An unsupported claim of realized savings is not. If the decision-makers won't attend, the session is preparation, not Executive Validation.

### Why sponsorship comes before validation
@src S74
Glean's Executive Readout training gives three reasons to secure executive sponsorship before any pilot or evaluation. You know what you are validating up front, so nothing is missed. You know there is budget. And you have agreement that if the validation succeeds, the customer will move forward into procurement and purchasing.

The same training notes that account teams can deliver the readout themselves with approved templates and the ROI calculator, bringing in value specialists for larger or more complex deals.

Figure: cards | What sponsorship secures | Three cards naming what executive sponsorship secures before validation: clarity on what will be validated, confirmation that budget exists, and agreement to move into procurement if validation succeeds.
- Clear scope :: You know what you're validating, and nothing is missed
- Budget :: There is money, or a path to it
- *A commitment :: Success leads to procurement and purchase

### Practice
Two days before the readout, the workflow maps are complete, but nobody owns the value inputs (time per case, population, frequency). Review readiness.

### Worked response
Not fully ready. Assign an owner for each value input, such as the support operations analyst for volumes and times, and review the assumptions with that owner before the readout. In the readout, state what remains directional and present the measurement plan.

Don't record the session as fully ready until sponsor attendance and decision coverage are confirmed.

## SBP-109-L02 Audience, sponsor and decision authority
@src S14 S02 S21

The right audience turns a presentation into a decision. Confirm who will be in the room and what each person can decide.

### Who should attend
@src S02 S14
@quote S02 | Key Players from DITL (dept heads, front-line employees who had strong reactions that were pro-Glean, etc...) AE & SE Glean Sales Leader if necessary
- **The executive sponsor:** confirms priority, investment support, and next-step authority.
- **The champion:** explains the work and adds customer credibility.
- **The technical champion:** validates dependencies and the evaluation plan.
- **Key participants from DITL:** department heads and front-line employees who can speak to the workflows firsthand.
- **The AE and SE owners**, plus a Glean sales leader if necessary.

### Sponsorship is not signature authority
Don't assume an enthusiastic end user, or even a champion, is the economic buyer. Confirm the actual decision process. Separate three things:
- **Sponsorship:** support for the initiative and its resources.
- **Budget authority:** the economic buyer.
- **Signature and procurement authority:** the paper process.

These may be different people, so record each one.

### When the executive doesn't come
If only the champion attends, use the meeting to prepare and refine the case, and then secure the sponsor's decision in a separate meeting. Don't log executive alignment as confirmed when it didn't happen.

### At a glance
Sponsorship, budget authority, and signature authority often sit with different people. Name each one.

Figure: stack | Three kinds of authority | Three stacked kinds of authority that often sit with different people: sponsorship, which backs the priority; budget authority, which funds it; and signature and procurement authority, which commits the organization.
- Sponsorship :: Backs the priority above the champion
- Budget authority :: Holds or can shift the budget
- *Signature and procurement :: Commits the organization

### Practice
Your planned executive readout is attended only by the champion. What do you do?

### Worked response
Use the time well. Walk the champion through the case, test the assumptions, and ask what the sponsor will challenge. Agree how the champion will help secure a 30-minute decision meeting with the sponsor this week or next.

Record the session as "preparation with the champion," not "executive alignment," and update the mutual action plan with the new decision date.

## SBP-109-L03 A decision-focused 60-minute readout
@src S02 S14

Executives give you an hour. Spend most of it on their workflows, their value, and the decision, not on a product tour.

### The narrative
@src S14
@quote S14 | Cover use‑case readout → future state → value → decision and next steps.
Glean's checklist frames the readout as a sequence: use case readout → future state → value → decision and next steps. A concise executive narrative covers:
1. The objective and context, including why this decision now.
2. The prioritized workflows, with before and after.
3. The business value and its assumptions.
4. Success criteria and the recommended validation path.
5. The decision and commitments.

### A suggested time plan
Table: A 60-minute readout (course design)
| Segment | Minutes |
|---|---|
| Context and objective | 5 |
| Workflow evidence: current vs. future, cost of inaction | 15 |
| Value: range, assumptions, evidence | 15 |
| Success criteria and validation recommendation | 10 |
| Decision and mutual action plan | 15 |

These timings are this course's design and fit a 60-minute session. They are not a mandated agenda.

### Keep the product reintroduction short
@src S02
@quote S02 | this should be no more than 5 minutes, more of a high-level overview of what Glean is and what value we provide to our clients.
Glean's playbook says repositioning Glean matters, especially with a new executive sponsor, but should take no more than five minutes. Give a high-level overview, then move to the customer's work.

### At a glance
A readout is a decision meeting. Most of the time goes to the customer's evidence and the decision, not the product.

Figure: flow | A decision-focused readout narrative | A five-part readout narrative: restate the three whys, play back what was heard, show the value range and assumptions, show a short tailored demonstration, and ask for the decision and validation path.
- The three whys :: In the customer's words
- What we heard :: Priorities and workflows from DITL
- Value and assumptions :: A range, with evidence labels
- A short tailored demo :: Only the priority workflows
- *The decision :: Sponsorship, criteria, and the validation path

### Practice
Your colleague's draft readout opens with a 40-slide product introduction. Cut it down to an executive opening.

### Worked response
Keep four things: the customer's problem in their words, why now, why Glean in this context (one slide, about five minutes at most), and the decision you'll ask for at the end.

Move the product background to an appendix or a separate link. Go straight to the customer's prioritized workflows and the evidence from DITL.

## SBP-109-L04 Present value honestly and invite challenge
@src S13 S19 S02

Executives trust a value case they can challenge. Present ranges, label evidence, and let the customer's own people speak to their experience.

### Connect evidence to business terms
@src S13
@quote S13 | Every ROI estimate must carry its assumptions, risk adjustments, and evidence tier transparently.
Link DITL evidence to cost, risk, or revenue. Show:
- **The value range:** conservative, base, and upside.
- **The material assumptions**, with their evidence level.
- **The plan to verify:** metric owners, baselines, and timing.

Let the champion or workflow owners confirm their experience in their own words. Don't speak for them.

### Invite challenge
State plainly that modeled capacity benefit is not confirmed P&L savings. Then ask:
- "Which assumptions do you accept?"
- "Which ones would you want validated?"
- "What outcome would justify the investment for you?"

The answers become the success criteria.

> **Boundary:** Don't make the optimistic scenario the headline without its caveats. Don't present internal benchmarks that aren't cleared for external use as independent proof.

### Anchor on the customer's own numbers
@src S73 S66
Glean's business-case training recommends presenting multiple ROI scenarios anchored in company-specific data. When an executive challenges an estimate, the strongest response is to show where each input came from and which assumption matters most, then agree how it will be checked. Glean's value-selling training adds that cost reduction is easier to quantify while growth drivers tend to have stronger executive impact, so be clear which numbers are which.

Figure: compare | Answering an executive challenge | A comparison of weak and strong responses when an executive challenges a value estimate. A weak response defends the number or cites a benchmark. A strong response shows the input sources, names the key assumption, and agrees how it will be measured.
- Weak response || Strong response
- Defends the headline number || Shows where each input came from
- Cites a generic benchmark || Uses the customer's own data
- Treats the range as a promise || Names the assumption that matters most
- Moves on quickly || Agrees how it will be measured

### Practice
Midway through the readout, the CFO says, "These are estimates, not savings." Respond.

### Worked response
"You're right, and that's how we've labeled them. This range is a pre-sales hypothesis based on your team's workflow interviews. These are the assumptions behind it, and this is the evidence for each. We propose agreeing now on the metric owner, the baseline, and the validation that would need to happen before anyone calls this value realized. Which assumptions would you most want tested?"

## SBP-109-L05 Secure the executive decision and validation path
@src S14 S15 S04

The readout should end with a decision: sponsorship, success criteria, scope, and the lightest validation that resolves what is still uncertain.

### What to confirm
@src S14
@quote S14 | Decide validation path (Glean Lab by default; Pilot/PoC only if necessary)
- **Sponsorship:** the executive will back the effort.
- **Success criteria:** each with a metric or quality threshold, an owner, an evidence source, and a timeframe.
- **Scope:** which workflows, teams, and data.
- **The next decision:** who decides, and when.

### Choose the lightest sufficient validation
Glean's Executive Validation checklist sets the default validation path as a Glean Lab, with a pilot or proof of concept only if necessary, to avoid unnecessary pilots and move faster. Ask: "What do you need to see to make a buy or no-buy decision?" Then choose the lightest event that answers that question. Often that is a tailored demo or a Glean Lab.

### Record conditional acceptance
Executives often accept part of a case. Record what was accepted, what is still open, and the blockers, each with an owner and a date.

> **Boundary:** A successful readout is not approval, and neither is a readiness score on a pilot checklist. Pilots go through Glean's internal review, and commercial and security steps stay separate.

### At a glance
Leave the readout with four things confirmed, or with a recorded condition and an owner for each gap.

Figure: cards | What to confirm before leaving the readout | Four cards naming what to confirm at the end of Executive Validation: sponsorship, success criteria, scope, and the next decision.
- Sponsorship :: Who backs it, and how they'll stay involved
- Success criteria :: Measurable, with owners
- Scope :: The workflows and teams in the validation
- *The next decision :: What happens if criteria are met

### Practice
The sponsor accepts the business priority and the directional value, but wants a permission-sensitive workflow tested first. Write the decision log entry.

### Worked response
- **Accepted:** the business priority, and the directional value range as a hypothesis.
- **Open:** whether restricted records behave correctly in the future-state workflow.
- **Next:** an SE-scoped validation with authorized test data and the customer's technical owner. Success criteria: restricted content is never returned to unauthorized test users, and the approved answer is found for the agreed scenarios. Decision date: [date].
- **Pilot:** only if the lighter validation can't resolve the question, and subject to internal approval.

## SBP-109-L06 After-work and opportunity update
@src S14 S46 S45

Momentum depends on fast, accurate follow-up. Commitments that aren't written down tend to drift.

### Same day to 24 hours
@src S14
@quote S14 | Send concise recap: decisions, success criteria, validation path, timeline, and owners
Glean's checklist asks for this within the same day to 24 hours after the meeting:
- **A concise recap:** decisions, success criteria, the validation path, the timeline, and owners.
- **Access** to the tailored demo or Glean Lab assets.
- **Updated records:** the opportunity workbook and stage criteria, reflecting the executive confirmations.
- **Business case and close plan:** built or refined.
- **Sponsor cadence:** the executive sponsor, technical champion, and partner engagement steps logged, and upcoming checkpoints confirmed.
- **Next validation:** if a pilot is needed, its scope, success metrics, and duration documented and kickoff scheduled. Otherwise, technical validation dates locked.

### Commitments versus open questions
The recap should separate accepted commitments from open questions. Link only approved, customer-facing assets. Reconfirm access permissions for every shared artifact.

> **Boundary:** Never send internal account judgments, scores, pricing policy, or private notes to the customer.

### At a glance
Send the recap within a day, and keep commitments separate from open questions.

Figure: flow | After the readout | A four-step after-work sequence within 24 hours: send a concise recap, update the opportunity records, refresh the business case and close plan, and set the sponsor cadence and next validation step.
- Send the recap :: Decisions, commitments, open questions
- Update records :: Opportunity and mutual action plan
- Refresh the business case :: With what the executive said
- *Set the cadence :: Sponsor check-ins and the next validation

### Practice
Draft the follow-up checklist for the day after Executive Validation.

### Worked response
1. Send the recap: decisions, success criteria, validation path, owners, and timeline.
2. Share the approved demo or Lab assets, and confirm access works.
3. Update the opportunity record and mutual action plan.
4. Log sponsor, champion, and partner actions and checkpoints.
5. Assign each open dependency an owner and date.
6. Schedule the validation event and the decision readout.
7. Start internal pilot approval only if a pilot is justified.
