# SBP-101 Blueprint Fundamentals

## SBP-101-L01 Purpose and customer promise
@src S02 S04 S06

Most enterprises have already experimented with AI. Many have run assistants, chatbots, or internal pilots. Far fewer can point to a result their finance team accepts: more revenue, lower cost, or lower risk for a specific part of the business. That gap is why the Blueprint Consultation exists.

### Why the Blueprint Consultation exists
@quote S02 | The time of experimentation is ending.
@quote S02 | Many companies have tested, experimented with, or even launched AI, but have not seen results directly tied to Return on Investment in the form of increased revenue, reduced costs, or reduced risk for a part of the business.
The Glean playbook describes the market plainly: the time of experimentation is ending. Buyers increasingly expect a new AI investment to prove the return they were promised, not just impress in a demo. "Pilot purgatory" is the failure mode you are trying to avoid: a long trial with no agreed success measure, no executive owner, and no decision at the end.

The Blueprint Consultation lets the account team partner with the customer early, before anyone discusses a pilot. Together you find the strongest use cases and work out how to show their value quickly.

### What it is
@quote S02 | The Blueprint Consultation is a series of meetings that happen with the client to create a defined process around evaluating and testing AI, helping to identify high- value workflows/ use cases with them, and generating alignment with the Executive Sponsor, all with the goal of only moving to a pilot when absolutely essential.
The Blueprint Consultation is a structured series of customer conversations. It creates a defined process for evaluating AI, identifies high-value workflows with the people who do the work, and builds alignment with an executive sponsor. The goal is to move to a pilot only when one is truly needed.

The playbook frames three jobs for the process:
- **Cut through ambiguity.** Anchor each use case to a business outcome, prioritize by impact and effort, set clear success metrics, and confirm executive sponsorship with mutual action plan checkpoints.
- **Validate value fast.** Quantify value from what you learn in Day-in-the-Life sessions, and plan for the change management that drives adoption.
- **Create budget-ready clarity.** Show where value comes from, including tool-consolidation offsets where they genuinely apply, in a form that fits the customer's governance and security needs.

### What it is not
It is not a free pilot, a general product tour, or an open-ended brainstorm. A compelling demo is not a validated business case. The deliverable is a decision-ready plan: prioritized workflows, an evidence-labeled value case, and an agreed way to validate. It is never a promise of guaranteed savings.

> **In plain language:** "We will agree the outcomes that matter to you, look at how the work happens today, and bring leadership an evidence-labeled value case and a recommendation for how to validate it."

### How to position it
@src S68 S69
@quote S68 | This will help us slow down to speed up.
Glean's internal Blueprint training is candid about a common mistake. Sellers who are uncomfortable with the process pitch it apologetically, and the customer hears "a lot of work." The stronger framing is consultative. The Blueprint is a structured analysis of where AI can change real work, run with the customer's own teams. Most business leaders can't easily do that analysis across their departments themselves.

The same training describes the Blueprint as a way to "slow down to speed up." Teams that jump straight to a pilot often stall later on scope, value, or sponsorship. A few focused sessions up front make the later decision faster and easier to defend.

Figure: compare | Two ways to start an AI evaluation | A comparison of a product-led start with a Blueprint-led start. The product-led start leads with features, a generic demo, and a trial request. The Blueprint-led start leads with business priorities, real workflows, measured value, and an executive decision.
- Product-led start || Blueprint-led start
- Leads with features and a generic demo || Leads with the customer's strategic priorities
- Use cases guessed by the seller || Use cases ranked with the business owners

### Practice
A champion asks, "What is this Blueprint thing, and why should my team give you time for it?" Write a two-sentence answer without product jargon. Then add one sentence on what remains uncertain at this point.

### Worked response
"We will identify and prioritize the workflows most tied to your business goals, then map how that work happens today and how it could work in future, with the people who do it. Together we will agree success measures and the lightest validation needed for an investment decision."

Uncertainty to name: until you have spoken with the business owners and end users, the value is a hypothesis, not a result.

## SBP-101-L02 Three-step operating sequence
@src S02 S04 S26

The Blueprint Consultation has three customer-facing steps. Preparation comes before them, and an agreed validation path and action plan comes after.

### The three steps
@quote S05 | Phase 1: Visioning Workshop
@quote S07 | Schedule a focused, 60-minute standalone visioning session ( do not combine with Glean intro demos or other meetings ).
@quote S05 | Phase 2: Day-in-the-Life (DITL) / Use Case Workshop
@quote S05 | Phase 3: Business Value Analysis / Executive Readout
1. **Visioning.** A session with business leadership, or the champion when leaders are not available. You align on priorities, brainstorm use cases tied to outcomes, and leave with a ranked shortlist of hero use cases. Glean guidance on length varies from 30 to 60 minutes. The Visioning playbook recommends a standalone 60-minute session that is not combined with an intro demo.
2. **Day-in-the-Life (DITL) interviews and playback.** One or more sessions with the leaders and front-line employees for each prioritized use case. You document how the work happens today, show what it could look like with Glean, and collect the inputs that size the value. Plan for 60 to 90 minutes per session.
3. **Executive Validation.** Glean materials also call this step the Executive Readout. Usually a 60-minute session with the executive sponsor. You confirm the value, the success criteria, sponsorship, and the validation path before more time and resources go into the evaluation.

Table: What each step produces
| Step | Main question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Visioning | Are there tangible business problems worth solving, and is there enough pain? | Prioritized use case list and a draft of success measures |
| Day-in-the-Life | What does the reality on the ground look like for three to four prioritized use cases? | Current-state and future-state workflow maps, metric inputs, data and connector needs |
| Executive Validation | Is there enough value to justify the investment, and how will we prove it? | Sponsor decision, success criteria, validation path, mutual action plan |

### Before and after the steps
@quote S04 | Before starting, you need: EB alignment , a value hypothesis, and identified PBOs aligned to the company/EB’s objective & near-term priority.
Preparation comes first. Glean guidance says that before you start, you need early economic buyer alignment, a value hypothesis, and identified Positive Business Outcomes (PBOs) tied to the company's near-term priorities.

Value analysis is not a separate customer meeting. It runs through the whole process: you form a hypothesis in preparation, test it in Visioning, size it with DITL data, and confirm it in Executive Validation.

After Executive Validation, the customer chooses how to validate the solution. The usual options are a tailored demo, a hands-on Glean Lab or sandbox, or a trial or proof of value. Glean's guidance is to prefer the lightest option that answers the customer's questions. Run a pilot only when it is genuinely necessary, and only after leadership agrees.

> **Watch for:** Skipping DITL and filling the business case with unrelated benchmarks. Without DITL, you have no customer-specific workflow evidence, so the value case cannot be defended.

### Three questions, three steps
@src S68 S04
@quote S68 | So each of these steps is going to answer a different question.
Glean's onboarding training summarizes the sequence simply: each step answers a different question. Visioning asks what matters to the business. DITL asks how the work actually happens today. Executive Validation asks whether there is enough value to invest. If you can't say which question you are answering, you are probably in the wrong step.

The training also notes that the sequence flexes by segment. A corporate deal may compress Visioning into one session. A strategic, wall-to-wall deal may run several Visioning sessions with different department heads and several DITL interviews. The order stays the same.

Figure: flow | Where the Blueprint sits in the sale | A five-step flow from Discovery to Visioning, then Day-in-the-Life interviews and playback, then Executive Validation, and finally a validation event chosen to answer the remaining buying question.
- Discovery :: Qualify the problem and the three whys
- Visioning :: What matters to the business?
- Day in the Life :: How does the work happen today?
- Executive Validation :: Is there enough value to invest?
- *Validation event :: Prove what is still uncertain

### Practice
Put these five items in order and explain in one line why each comes where it does: executive decision, DITL interviews, validation event, Visioning, preparation.

### Worked response
Preparation → Visioning → DITL interviews and value analysis → Executive Validation (executive decision) → agreed validation event and mutual action plan.

Preparation gives you a hypothesis to test. Visioning narrows the field to a few hero use cases. DITL supplies the workflow evidence and metric inputs. The executive decides with that evidence in hand, and only then is a validation event scheduled.

## SBP-101-L03 Value vocabulary
@src S05 S06 S13

Blueprint conversations fall apart when "outcome", "use case", "workflow", and "metric" are used interchangeably. Keep them separate. Each answers a different question, and an executive case needs all four.

### Four terms, four questions
@src S05 S13
@quote S05 | A simple discovery framework: business outcomes → use cases → workflows → data → success metrics.
Table: The value vocabulary
| Term | Question it answers | Example (fictional support team) |
|---|---|---|
| Positive Business Outcome (PBO) | What improves for the business, and who owns it? | Lower support cost without a drop in resolution quality, owned by the VP of Support |
| Use case | What job needs doing? | Find approved troubleshooting guidance for a live case |
| Workflow | What are the steps, people, and systems involved today? | Case opens → agent searches three systems → asks a peer → drafts a reply → reply is reviewed |
| Metric | How will we judge improvement? | Average handle time, paired with resolution quality and escalation rate |

Glean's partner discovery framework chains these in one direction: business outcomes → use cases → workflows → data → success metrics. Start at the outcome, not the tool.

### Connect to cost, revenue, or risk
@src S13 S02
@quote S13 | Time saved is an input, not an outcome.
Business impact should connect to cost, revenue, or risk, because those are what executive sponsors fund. The Glean Value Playbook puts it directly: time saved is an input, not an outcome. The outcome is what happens with the time: revenue influenced, costs avoided, risks reduced, capacity redeployed, or quality improved.

Compare two statements. "Save 30 minutes a day" is an activity claim. "Cut RFP response time so the team can answer more RFPs each quarter" is a value story, because it names a business result and a way to measure it.

### Activities are not outcomes
Meetings held, agent ideas generated, and connectors added are activities or enablers. They may be necessary, but they are not outcomes. "Fewer clicks" is not automatically an outcome either. To make it one, name the business owner and the operational or financial consequence.

> **Test yourself:** If you can't say who would notice the improvement and what it would change on their scorecard, you have an activity, not an outcome.

### Pass the "who cares?" test
@src S71 S66
@quote S71 | value starts at day one.
Glean's business-case training pushes one test hard: who cares? A faster slide deck matters to its author but not to a CFO. A CFO cares about revenue, cost, and risk. The training's message is that value starts at day one. If the first conversations anchor on personal productivity, the deal is later compared with cheap point tools.

Use the vocabulary to climb from the activity to the outcome an executive would fund. If you can't name who would care about a metric and why, keep climbing.

Figure: stack | From job to business outcome | Four stacked layers showing how the value vocabulary connects. At the top is the Positive Business Outcome, then the metric that measures it, then the workflow where the work happens, and at the base the use case, the job that needs doing.
- *Positive Business Outcome :: What improves for the business, and who funds it
- Metric :: How improvement will be judged
- Workflow :: The steps, people, and systems involved today
- Use case :: The job that needs doing

### Practice
A customer says, "We need an AI assistant." Translate that into an outcome, a use case, a workflow, and a metric. Use the support example or a fictional team of your choice.

### Worked response
- **Outcome:** Lower support cost without lowering resolution quality.
- **Use case:** Retrieve approved resolutions for live cases.
- **Workflow:** Case context → search → review the suggested answer → respond.
- **Metric:** Handle time, paired with resolution quality and escalation rate so speed does not come at the cost of quality.

Uncertainty to name: you still need a baseline for handle time and confirmation that the approved resolutions exist in systems Glean can reach.

## SBP-101-L04 Customer, Glean and partner value
@src S05 S06 S26

A Blueprint should be worth the time for everyone in the room. When each party knows what it gains and what it is accountable for, the engagement moves faster and handoffs stay clean.

### What each party gains
@src S26 S05
- **The customer** gets business-level alignment and an actionable roadmap: prioritized use cases, current-state evidence, business-aligned metrics, and a group-level business case. The process also builds excitement and sponsorship across business units, which helps secure budget and resources.
- **Glean** gets a better-qualified, value-led evaluation with a named sponsor and clear success criteria, rather than an open-ended trial.
- **The partner** brings industry knowledge, facilitation skill, and technical context. In return, the Blueprint creates a clear delivery roadmap that can turn discovery into adoption, agent builds, and ongoing optimization.

### Who does what across the lifecycle
@src S05
@quote S05 | Partner owns day-to-day agent builds and optimization, while Glean provides patterns, technical guidance, and product support.
@quote S05 | Glean AE/SE: Accountable for renewal and expansion
Glean's partner enablement material sets out a responsibility split for partner-led engagements:

Table: Partner and Glean responsibilities (partner enablement RACI snapshot)
| Activity | Partner | Glean |
|---|---|---|
| Use case discovery | Facilitates workshops with business stakeholders | Shares patterns and technical constraints |
| Build | Leads day-to-day agent builds | Coaches, with patterns and technical guidance |
| Optimize and ongoing support | Front-line advisory and small changes | Glean Support handles product issues |
| Business reviews and renewals | Brings the AI outcomes story, services roadmap, and joint next-step proposals | Glean AE and SE are accountable for renewal and expansion |

### Accountability boundaries
Partner participation does not transfer commercial approval, product support accountability, or permission to describe capabilities Glean has not confirmed. Agree who owns what before the first session: who facilitates, who takes notes, who answers product feasibility questions, and who owns the follow-up. Glean keeps its own processes for approving pilots and escalating product issues.

> **Good use of partner expertise:** A partner leads a departmental workshop and translates the customer's goals into grounded workflow discovery. The Glean account team supplies product patterns and feasibility guidance. The result is a clearer services scope, not just more workshops.

### At a glance
Each party gets something different from the same engagement. Seeing the three side by side helps you explain it.

- The customer :: Prioritized use cases and a business case they can defend internally
- Glean :: A qualified opportunity with sponsorship and a clear validation path
- *The partner :: A services roadmap tied to measured customer outcomes

### Practice
Name one benefit for the customer, one for the partner, and one for Glean in a Blueprint you might run. Then state one accountability boundary you would agree before the first session.

### Worked response
- **Customer:** Clear priorities and evidence for an investment decision.
- **Partner:** A relevant, well-defined services scope that follows from the prioritized use cases.
- **Glean:** A qualified evaluation with a named sponsor and agreed success criteria.
- **Boundary:** Glean keeps its internal pilot-approval and product-escalation processes. The partner does not commit to pilots, pricing, or roadmap items on Glean's behalf.

## SBP-101-L05 Sales Blueprint versus solution architecture
@src S02 S51 S54

"Blueprint" means two different things at Glean. Keep them separate so customers know which conversation they are in.

### Two kinds of blueprint
@src S51 S02
@quote S51 | The Customer Blueprint Framework provides SA-led, opinionated patterns for solving complex post-sales challenges.
- **The Sales Blueprint (Blueprint Consultation)** is a pre-sales process. It covers discovery, workflow prioritization, business value, and the evaluation decision. Its outputs include a prioritized use case list, current-state and future-state workflow maps, an evidence-labeled value case, and an executive validation decision.
- **The Customer Blueprint Framework** is a post-sales program led by Glean Solutions Architecture. Each blueprint is a reusable package for a recurring post-sales challenge: a problem statement, reference architecture and integration patterns, a recommended implementation path and governance model, and example assets. Examples include patterns for building many custom connectors and for optimizing infrastructure cost.

### How they relate
Architecture patterns can inform feasibility and delivery. If DITL shows that a use case depends on several custom connectors, a post-sales pattern may shape the effort estimate and the handoff plan. But an architecture pattern does not replace customer discovery or executive alignment. A connector pattern explains how something can be built. The Sales Blueprint explains why it matters to a specific team, how their work changes, and how success will be measured.

> **Boundary:** Do not present architecture diagrams as proof of value. This accreditation does not replace technical architecture or connector accreditation.

### At a glance
The two artifacts share a word but answer different questions. Keep them separate in your notes and your scope.

Figure: compare | Two kinds of blueprint | A comparison of the Sales Blueprint, which decides whether and where to invest, with the Customer Blueprint Framework, which designs how the solution will be built and run.
- Sales Blueprint || Customer Blueprint Framework
- Should the customer invest, and where? || How will the solution be built and run?
- Use cases, workflows, value, executive decision || Architecture, data sources, governance, rollout
- Pre-sale, led by the account team || Delivery planning, led by technical owners

### Practice
Classify each item as a Sales Blueprint output or an adjacent technical-delivery asset: a prioritized use case list, an implementation reference architecture, and an executive value readout.

### Worked response
- **Prioritized use case list:** Sales Blueprint output (from Visioning).
- **Executive value readout:** Sales Blueprint output (from Executive Validation).
- **Implementation reference architecture:** An adjacent technical-delivery asset. It may be linked in the handoff when it informs feasibility, but it is not evidence that the customer will get value.

## SBP-101-L06 Quality gates and scope of this course
@src S02 S13 S14 S54 S55

A Blueprint is judged by the decision it enables, not by the number of meetings held. Use the checklist below before any executive readout. This course asks you to produce these outputs and show the evidence behind them.

### What a credible Blueprint contains
@src S13 S14 S02
@quote S14 | Current vs. Future workflows documented for prioritized use cases
@quote S14 | Meeting objectives and success criteria defined
- A documented outcome hypothesis, tied to a named business priority and a business owner.
- Prioritized workflows, with the rationale for impact and effort.
- Current-state and future-state workflow maps for each prioritized use case.
- Transparent value assumptions, labeled by evidence type, with risk adjustments.
- Success criteria and a plan for the baseline, meaning how and when the "before" will be measured.
- A recommended validation path that is the lightest option able to answer the customer's open questions.
- An agreed next decision, with owners and dates in a mutual action plan.

### Evidence standards
@src S13
@quote S13 | If you don't know the "before," the "after" is meaningless.
@quote S13 | Every pre-sales claim must identify the evidence source that will validate it.
Pre-sales value is only credible if there is a measurable path to prove it later. Every claim should name the evidence that will validate it. Survey data alone is directional. Corroborate it with system data or product telemetry where you can. A missing baseline is an action to resolve, not a number to invent.

> **Audit habit:** Before the executive readout, check that every top use case has a business owner, its data dependencies, a baseline plan, and a value rationale. Treat any gap as an open action with an owner.

### Scope of this course
This preview teaches and assesses the outputs above using fictional scenarios. It requires evidence, not attendance alone: each module ends with a check, and the practical is scored by a qualified human assessor. The proposed credential name, AI Sales Blueprinting Specialist (AISBS), and its pass rules are instructional-design proposals. They are not approved Glean policy, and the program owner must confirm them before release.

### At a glance
A credible Blueprint holds up when someone who wasn't in the room reads it. Use these four checks before any readout.

### Practice
A colleague's draft for an executive readout contains only a demo deck and a headline: "Projected savings: $2M per year." Audit it. What would you return, and what must be added?

### Worked response
Return it for completion. A demo alone is not a business case, and the savings headline has no stated assumptions, evidence, or owner. Add:
- The prioritized workflows, with current-state and future-state evidence from DITL.
- Named business and technical owners.
- The value model with each assumption labeled by evidence type, plus a risk adjustment.
- A measurement plan, including the baseline.
- The sponsor decision being requested, the recommended validation path, and the mutual action plan.
