# SBP-113 Facilitation, Objections and Pitfalls

## SBP-113-L01 Facilitate with structure and neutrality
@src S07 S02 S05

Good facilitation gives the conversation a clear structure and then lets the customer do most of the talking.

### Set the tone
@src S07
@quote S07 | Set an open, consultative, and strategic tone—avoid checklist-style or interrogative discovery.
Glean's Visioning playbook asks for an open, consultative, and strategic tone, and warns against checklist-style or interrogative discovery. In practice:
- **Open** by stating the purpose, the agenda, the decision you need, and the time.
- **Assign roles:** a facilitator, a note-taker, a timekeeper, and a technical owner for capability questions.
- **Use open questions** and play back what you heard so it can be corrected.
- **Make the work visible:** effort-and-impact tables, a whiteboard, and sample scenarios.

### Neutrality protects the evidence
Ask the question, then let the customer answer it. Paraphrase what they said instead of reframing it into a product requirement. Don't treat silence or politeness as agreement.

> **Boundary:** Don't commit to features, timelines, or roadmap items during a workshop. Capture capability questions for the right technical owner.

### At a glance
Neutral facilitation protects the evidence. Leading questions get you agreement, not information.

Figure: compare | Leading versus open questions | A comparison of leading questions with open alternatives. For example, asking whether Glean agents would make the process faster becomes asking what slows the process down today.
- Leading || Open
- Wouldn't agents make this faster? || What slows this process down today?
- This is your biggest problem, right? || Which problem costs the most, and why?
- You'd use this every day? || When did this last happen, and what did you do?

### Practice
Rewrite this question: "Wouldn't Glean agents make your process much faster?"

### Worked response
"Walk us through the last time this process was slow. Where did the time go? What information or approvals were involved, and how would you know a change had worked?"

## SBP-113-L02 Manage participation and dominant voices
@src S07 S22 S02

The loudest voice in the room isn't always the best evidence. Structure the session so that every relevant role contributes.

### Keep groups focused
@src S07
@quote S07 | Limit participant size (ideally 1-3 per session) to maximize candor and focus; run additional sessions by line of business if needed.
Glean's Visioning playbook recommends small sessions, ideally one to three participants, to encourage candor and focus, with additional sessions by line of business if needed. When a larger group can't be avoided:
- Use structured turns, so each role answers the same question.
- Ask for silent writing before discussion.
- Invite specific roles: "What does this look like for a new agent?"
- Run separate, role-specific follow-ups.

### Dominance and the parking lot
A senior leader's opinion matters. It isn't the same as a front-line workflow observation, so record both and label them. When someone pulls toward their pet topic, park it visibly with an owner and return to the agenda.

> **Boundary:** Never put participants on the spot about sensitive issues. Respect consent, privacy, and organizational norms.

### At a glance
When one voice dominates, change the format rather than confronting the person.

Figure: cards | Techniques for balanced participation | Four cards naming techniques for balanced participation: direct questions to specific roles, use silent writing before discussion, use a parking lot for off-topic items, and follow up separately with quieter participants.
- Direct by role :: Ask the people who do the work
- Silent writing first :: Everyone contributes before discussion
- *Parking lot :: Capture off-topic items, with an owner
- Separate follow-up :: Hear quieter voices one to one

### Practice
A senior manager answers every question meant for front-line agents. What do you do?

### Worked response
Thank them and capture their view. Then run a structured round: "I'd like to hear from each of you about the last access case you handled." If needed, schedule a separate, consented interview with the agents.

Record which input was observed behavior and which was a leadership view.

## SBP-113-L03 Handle evidence and value objections
@src S13 S19 S02

Value objections are a test of credibility. Answer them with evidence and transparency, and don't get defensive or lean on salesmanship.

### The common challenges
@src S13
@quote S13 | Always identify which assumptions are benchmark-based vs. customer-specific
Glean's Value Playbook prepares teams for the most frequent CFO and CIO challenges, such as "Where did the saved time go?" and "These are just benchmarks." It also asks teams to always identify which assumptions are benchmark-based and which are customer-specific.

Table: Responding to value objections
| You hear | Respond with |
|---|---|
| "These are just estimates." | Agreement, the labeled assumptions, and the validation plan |
| "Where does the saved time go?" | The recapture and reallocation assumptions, and their owner |
| "Those are just benchmarks." | Which inputs are customer-specific, and how benchmark inputs will be replaced with measured data |
| "We don't believe the survey." | Corroboration from telemetry and system-of-record data |

### The pattern
1. **Acknowledge** the concern.
2. **Ask** what evidence would change their view.
3. **Show** the assumptions, the range, and the current evidence level.
4. **Agree** on a test, with an owner and a date.

> **Boundary:** Don't defend an inflated number. Revise it in front of them if they're right.

### Give the champion a story they can carry
@src S65
@quote S65 | Am I giving them a strong enough story to go sell this internally?
Glean's value-selling training is a reminder of why objection handling matters: assume Glean is not a budgeted initiative. When a finance leader challenges your numbers, the champion will have to answer the same challenge later, without you. Handle the objection so the champion leaves with an answer they can repeat.

Figure: flow | Handling an evidence or value objection | A four-step pattern for handling objections: acknowledge the concern, ask what evidence would resolve it, show the source and assumptions, and agree how it will be checked.
- Acknowledge :: The concern is reasonable
- Ask :: What evidence would resolve it?
- Show :: The source, range, and assumptions
- *Agree :: How and when it will be checked

### Practice
A finance leader says, "Saved hours aren't savings." Respond.

### Worked response
"Agreed. On its own, saved time is capacity, not cash. Here is what we've assumed about how much of that time is actually redeployed, and to what work. We'd propose that you or your operations owner confirm the reallocation plan, and that we measure the business metric it should move, before anyone calls it savings. Which part of the model would you want to test first?"

## SBP-113-L04 Handle readiness, data and pilot objections
@src S15 S16 S05

Some objections are about readiness: data, security, scope, and the evaluation itself. Respond by naming the dependency and the right next step, without overpromising.

### Typical readiness objections
- **"We're not sure our data is ready."** Agree on a readiness check for the priority sources and their owners.
- **"Security will never approve this."** Engage the security owner early, and use Glean's approved security materials and review process.
- **"Just give us a free pilot."** Ask which buying question a pilot would answer, then propose the lightest validation that answers it.

### The pilot request
@src S15
@quote S15 | No approval = no pilot.
Glean's pilot guide is explicit that there is no pilot without approval. A pilot requires a real sponsor, a scoped buying question, measurable criteria, readiness evidence, and internal approval.

> **Boundary:** Never promise a pilot, its environment, timeline, or terms. Partners don't approve or provision pilots.

### Handle "just give us a pilot"
@src S67 S68
Glean's validation-events training explains that pilots convert because the groundwork is done first: security review, the Blueprint, an economic buyer's stated priorities, executive buy-in, budget conversations, and IT readiness. Use that to reframe a pilot request. Agree on the question the pilot must answer, then show what has to be in place for the pilot to lead to a decision.

Figure: compare | Reframing a pilot request | A comparison of a broad pilot request with a focused reframe. A broad request asks to see what Glean can do, for everyone, open-ended. The reframe defines the question, a cohort, the priority workflows, success criteria, and a decision date.
- Broad request || Focused reframe
- See what it can do || Answer one buying question
- Everyone, all content || A defined cohort and data
- Open-ended duration || Fixed duration and decision date
- No success criteria || Measurable criteria agreed first

### Practice
The customer insists on a broad 60-day pilot "to see what it can do." Respond.

### Worked response
"We want you to see real value, so let's make sure an evaluation answers a decision you need to make. Which specific workflows and outcomes would you need to see to decide? Let's start there with a focused validation, such as a tailored demo or a Lab scenario. If a question remains that only your own data can answer, we'll scope a pilot properly, with criteria and owners, and take it through Glean's approval process."

## SBP-113-L05 Avoid common Blueprint pitfalls
@src S05 S13 S02

Most failed Blueprints fail in predictable ways. Recognize the pattern early and correct course.

### Common pitfalls
Table: Common pitfalls and corrections
| Pitfall | Correction |
|---|---|
| Product tour instead of discovery | Lead with the customer's workflows and outcomes |
| A long wishlist, no priorities | Score, rank, and pick a small set of heroes |
| Generic DITL steps | Interview real participants about real recent cases |
| Benchmarks presented as proof | Label the evidence; measure the customer's baseline |
| Readout with no decision-maker | Treat it as preparation; secure the sponsor separately |
| A pilot as the default | Choose the lightest sufficient validation |
| The value case lost at handoff | Metric cards, owners, and an accepted handoff |

### Diagnose before you fix
For each pitfall, ask what evidence or decision is missing, who owns it, and what the smallest corrective step is.

### Pitch it with confidence
@src S68 S69
Glean's onboarding training highlights a pitfall that happens before the Blueprint starts. Teams that lack confidence in the process present it as a burden, and customers decline. The fix is to position it consultatively, as a structured way to find where AI will matter most, and to show what the customer gets at the end.

Figure: cards | Common Blueprint pitfalls | Six cards naming common Blueprint pitfalls: pitching the Blueprint apologetically, Visioning without prioritization, DITL skipped before a trial, value claimed without baselines, a readout with no decision, and a pilot with no success criteria.
- Apologetic pitch :: Positioned as a burden
- Endless Visioning :: Ideas, but no priorities
- DITL skipped :: Straight to a trial
- Value without baselines :: Numbers no one owns
- Readout without a decision :: A presentation, not a meeting
- *Pilot without criteria :: A test with no path to a decision

### Practice
Diagnose this situation: three Visioning sessions have produced 40 use cases, but no business owner has prioritized any of them.

### Worked response
This is the wishlist pitfall. Run a scoring session with the business owners to select a small set of hero use cases. Each hero needs an owner, an outcome, and a target, and the rest stay in a backlog.

Confirm the hero choices with the sponsor before scheduling DITL interviews.

## SBP-113-L06 Recover a stalled engagement
@src S12 S05 S14

Blueprint engagements stall when the urgency, the sponsor, or the next step disappears. Recover by diagnosing which one is missing, not by sending more material.

### Diagnose the stall
- **Urgency:** is the business problem still a priority? Has something changed?
- **Sponsor:** is the executive still engaged? Has the champion lost access or influence?
- **Next step:** is there an agreed, dated action with an owner?
- **Evidence:** is the case blocked by a missing baseline, a source, or a feasibility question?

### Act on the diagnosis
Re-engage the champion with a specific, useful offer, such as a short recap of the agreed priorities with a proposed next decision. Confirm or replace the sponsor path. If the priority has really changed, record that honestly. A clear "not now" is better than a false forecast.

> **Boundary:** Don't flood the customer with generic content or invent urgency. Update the opportunity record accurately.

### At a glance
Name the cause before choosing the fix. Each of the four causes has a different recovery.

Figure: matrix | Diagnosing a stalled engagement | A two-by-two grid of the four causes of a stall: urgency, the sponsor, the next step, and the evidence. Each quadrant names the recovery action.
- x: Commitment gap
- y: Clarity gap
- tl: Urgency :: Revisit why now with the sponsor
- tr: Sponsor :: Re-engage or find a new sponsor
- bl: Next step :: Propose one specific, owned step
- br: Evidence :: Close the gap with the smallest session

### Practice
The champion has gone quiet for three weeks after DITL interviews. Write your recovery plan.

### Worked response
1. Send the champion a concise draft of the DITL findings for their team, with one question: "Is this still the priority, and what would help you take it to the sponsor?"
2. Offer a short working session to finalize the findings together.
3. If there's no response, ask the AE to check the sponsor path, for example through the executive relationship.
4. Update the MAP and the forecast to reflect what is actually known.
