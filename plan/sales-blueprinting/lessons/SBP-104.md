# SBP-104 Prepare and Hypothesize

## SBP-104-L01 Build the account evidence pack
@src S05 S25 S36 S37

Good Blueprints are won before the first workshop. Preparation turns what you already know into a short, sourced pack. It tells the team what is confirmed, what is assumed, and what still needs to be asked.

### What to gather
Draw only on account context you are permitted to use:
- The customer's strategy and stated initiatives, such as annual reports, investor materials, and leadership statements.
- Industry pressures and regulation that shape priorities.
- Stakeholders and their known goals, from prior meetings and the champion.
- Existing tools and systems, and any known pain with them.
- Notes from previous calls, in the customer's own words where possible.

### Label every fact by its origin
Separate four kinds of information, and record the date and access limits for each:

Table: Kinds of information in a prep pack
| Kind | Example | How to treat it |
|---|---|---|
| Customer statement | "Our VP said handle time is the top support KPI." (call notes, March) | Strongest; still confirm it is current |
| Public research | An annual report names cost efficiency as a priority | Context; may be dated or high-level |
| Internal hypothesis | "Support agents probably search several systems per case." | Test in Visioning and DITL |
| Unknown | Who owns the support budget | An open question with an owner |

### A simple four-part structure
1. **Verified priorities:** what the customer has said matters, with sources and dates.
2. **Stakeholder map:** who decides, who informs, who owns metrics, and who controls dependencies.
3. **Systems and data landscape:** known tools, likely content sources, and access constraints.
4. **Open questions:** what you must learn, and from whom.

Every customer fact should link to its source, not to a generated summary of it.

> **Boundary:** Don't move restricted customer data into a partner workspace, and don't present a peer customer's story as this prospect's expected result. Use only authorized tools and sources.

### Fit the narrative to the organization
@src S66
@quote S66 | Adapt your value narrative to your prospect's organizational life cycle for maximum relevance.
Glean's value-selling training adds a useful lens for research: different organizations prioritize different kinds of value. Startups tend to focus on growth, mid-stage companies on efficiency, and large enterprises on risk and differentiation. Use these as prompts for what to look for, then confirm what this customer actually says.

The same training notes that most use cases deliver several value drivers at once, and that strong business cases combine growth and cost reduction. Cost reduction is easier to quantify. Growth drivers tend to have stronger executive impact.

Figure: cards | Label every fact by its origin | Four cards for the four kinds of account evidence: customer statements, which are the strongest; public research, which needs a date; internal hypotheses, which must be validated; and unknowns, which become open questions with owners.
- *Customer statement :: Strongest; record who said it and when
- Public research :: Useful context; note its date
- Internal hypothesis :: Label it; validate before relying on it
- Unknown :: An open question with an owner

### Practice
Your call notes say the customer's top priority is "faster customer support." The annual report, published eight months earlier, emphasizes "cost discipline across operations." Outline the prep pack entry for priorities.

### Worked response
- **Priority A:** faster customer support. Evidence: call notes with the support director, with the date. A customer statement.
- **Priority B:** cost discipline across operations. Evidence: annual report, eight months old. Public research.
- **Conflict flagged:** these may be the same initiative described differently, since faster support can lower cost per case, or two competing priorities.
- **Action:** ask the champion in Visioning prep which initiative is current and who owns it. Keep the value hypothesis conditional until the champion confirms.

## SBP-104-L02 Why Change, Why Now, Why Glean
@src S04 S06 S36

A value hypothesis is an informed point of view that you bring to the customer to test. It is not a claim that discovery is complete. The three whys give it structure.

### The three whys
@src S04 S16
@quote S04 | The output is a value hypothesis identified pain, champions, and access to power within the
@quote S16 | Why Change? – What changes if they do nothing?
- **Why change?** What is the business problem, and what happens if the customer does nothing?
- **Why now?** Why is it a priority this quarter or year, rather than someday?
- **Why Glean?** Why might Glean be relevant to this problem in this customer's environment?

In Glean's sales process, discovery is AE-led and produces a value hypothesis, identified pain, champions, and access to power. The Blueprint then tests and refines that hypothesis with the people who do the work.

### Keep the causal claim honest
Separate what the customer has confirmed, such as "service cost is under review," from what you are proposing, such as "unified access to approved knowledge might reduce search time." The proposal is the part you will test. Phrase it as a hypothesis and attach a feasibility check.

> **Example (fictional support team):** **Why change:** fragmented guidance across several systems increases support effort and inconsistency. **Why now:** service cost is under formal review this year. **Why Glean:** permission-aware access to relevant knowledge across existing systems may make approved answers easier to find. This depends on the content being in reachable systems, which we will validate.

### Avoid feature lists
A list of capabilities is not a hypothesis. If your "Why Glean" would read the same for any customer, it isn't specific enough. Tie it to the systems, people, and workflow you expect to find.

### Use them in every meeting
@src S68
Glean's onboarding training notes that many teams open every customer meeting with the three whys, then move to the executive summary. By the executive readout, the three whys are no longer the seller's hypothesis. They are restated from what the customer said in Visioning and DITL.

Figure: flow | The three whys | A three-step sequence: why change, which names the business problem and its cost; why now, which names the trigger and deadline; and why Glean, which names the differentiators that matter for this problem.
- Why change? :: The problem and what it costs today
- Why now? :: The trigger, deadline, or priority that makes it urgent
- *Why Glean? :: The differentiators that matter for this problem

### Practice
Draft the three whys for a fictional IT service desk that handles employee questions about laptops, access, and software.

### Worked response
- **Why change:** agents repeat the same searches and escalate questions that existing articles already answer, so employees wait longer.
- **Why now:** IT leadership wants to absorb a headcount freeze without lowering service levels this fiscal year (to confirm with the IT director).
- **Why Glean:** test whether unified, permission-aware access to approved knowledge reduces search effort and avoidable escalations, while keeping answer quality and access controls in place.

Uncertainty: we don't yet know where the approved knowledge lives, or how current it is.

## SBP-104-L03 PBOs and metric hypotheses
@src S06 S34 S13

Positive Business Outcomes (PBOs) translate strategy into results a business owner would recognize. Metrics make them measurable. In preparation, both are hypotheses.

### From priority to outcome to metric
@src S13 S34
@quote S34 | DON'T use the same metrics for every persona.
For each relevant line of business, draft one or two PBOs and three to five candidate metrics. Glean's Metrics Library groups metrics by function and warns against using the same metrics for every persona. A CIO cares about different measures than a sales director.

For each metric, write down three things:
- **What** is measured: a precise definition, such as "mean handle time for password and access cases."
- **How** it will be measured: the system, the time window, and who supplies the data.
- **Why** it connects to the outcome: the logic linking the metric to the PBO.

### Balance speed with quality
@src S13
Choose a small, balanced set. A speed metric needs a quality guardrail, so that "faster" doesn't turn into "faster but worse." For support, a balanced set might pair handle time with resolution rate, escalation rate, and cost per resolved case.

Table: A balanced metric set (fictional support team)
| Metric | Type | Why it is in the set |
|---|---|---|
| Mean handle time (agreed case class) | Efficiency | Captures effort per case |
| First-contact resolution rate | Quality guardrail | Shows answers are still right |
| Escalation rate to tier 2 | Quality and cost | Detects pushed-down complexity |
| Cost per resolved case | Financial | Connects to the cost outcome |

> **Boundary:** These are hypotheses until the metric owners confirm definitions, baselines, and feasibility. Never borrow an industry benchmark and label it the customer's current state.

### Ask what they track today
@src S65
Glean's value-selling training highlights the question that often matters most: "What are you tracking today?" Metrics the customer already measures are easier to baseline and more credible to finance. If a strong champion already has an analysis for their team, build on it rather than starting from scratch.

Figure: stack | A balanced metric set | Four stacked metrics for a fictional support team: mean handle time for efficiency, first-contact resolution as a quality guardrail, escalation rate for quality and cost, and cost per resolved case for the financial view.
- Mean handle time :: Efficiency; captures effort per case
- First-contact resolution :: Quality guardrail
- Escalation rate :: Detects work pushed to tier 2
- *Cost per resolved case :: Connects to the financial outcome

### Practice
Write one complete metric hypothesis for the fictional IT service desk, using What, How, and Why.

### Worked response
- **What:** mean handle time for access-request tickets.
- **How:** compare consistent open-to-resolve timestamps in the IT service-management system over an agreed baseline period and a matching validation period. The service-desk manager supplies the export.
- **Why:** if agents find approved procedures faster, effort per ticket should fall and free capacity, provided first-contact resolution stays stable.

Uncertainty: the definition of the ticket class and the data export must be confirmed by the metric owner.

## SBP-104-L04 Sponsor and readiness pressure test
@src S04 S07 S02 S18

Before Visioning, test whether the session can succeed. A workshop without the basics wastes the customer's goodwill and produces a use case list nobody owns.

### What to confirm
@src S07 S04
@quote S07 | Decline or reschedule if pre-work, survey responses, or executive sponsor alignment are missing.
- Sponsor alignment, or a credible and documented path to it.
- Completed pre-work from the customer.
- The relevant stakeholders invited, with the right lines of business represented.
- A clear outcome for the session, an agenda, and working facilitation tools.

Glean's Visioning playbook is direct: decline or reschedule if pre-work, survey responses, or executive sponsor alignment are missing.

### Three pressure-test questions
Ask the champion:
1. "Which initiative does this work support?"
2. "Who owns the KPI we would move?"
3. "Who can sponsor the next investment decision?"

If the answers are vague, schedule a narrower alignment conversation before the full workshop.

### Go, narrow, or reschedule
- **Go:** basics are in place.
- **Narrow:** some basics are missing but the meeting is valuable. Convert it into a qualification and alignment conversation, and say so.
- **Reschedule:** pre-work is empty and the sponsor path is unknown.

> **Note on sponsor attendance:** Glean sources differ on whether the executive sponsor should attend early Visioning. Champion-led Visioning can work, but Executive Validation must still resolve executive alignment. Don't turn a preferred attendance pattern into a universal rule.

### At a glance
The readiness review ends in one of three decisions. Make the call explicitly, and tell the champion why.

Figure: compare | Go, narrow, or reschedule | A comparison of the three readiness decisions with when each applies: go when the sponsor, priorities, and attendees are confirmed; narrow when only part of the scope is ready; reschedule when the decision-makers or business owners can't attend.
- Decision || When it applies
- Go || Sponsor, priorities, and the right attendees are confirmed
- Narrow || Only one team or use case area is ready
- *Reschedule || Decision-makers or business owners can't attend

### Practice
Two days before Visioning, the worksheet is still empty and the champion can't say who would sponsor an investment. Decide whether to go, narrow, or reschedule, and assign owners.

### Worked response
Reschedule the full workshop, or narrow the slot to a qualification and alignment conversation with the champion. The AE owns the sponsor path and collecting the pre-work, with a new date agreed. Don't record the readiness gate as passed. Note in the action plan what must be in place before the workshop is rebooked.

## SBP-104-L05 Pre-work, agenda and invitation
@src S08 S09 S07

The invitation sets expectations. A clear one brings the right people, prepared, to a session with a defined outcome.

### What the invitation should say
@src S08
@quote S08 | please complete the attached pre-work worksheet by [Due Date: 2 days before session]
- **Purpose:** align AI work with the customer's business outcomes.
- **Expected outcomes:** prioritized use cases, named owners, and next steps for deeper workflow interviews.
- **Attendees and roles:** who will facilitate and who will capture outputs.
- **Preparation:** the pre-work worksheet, due two days before the session in Glean's template.
- **The next decision:** what happens after the session.

### What the pre-work asks for
@src S08
Glean's template asks customers to cover their department's core goals and success metrics, current workflow challenges and bottlenecks, desired business outcomes, and stakeholder priorities. A practical worksheet has one row per candidate workflow, with columns for the systems used, how often the work happens, the time it takes, and the pain it causes. A few relevant example use cases help as thought starters. Tell customers they can edit or discard them.

### A 60-minute agenda
@src S08
@quote S08 | Use Case Prioritization (15-20 min): Rank opportunities by business impact and effort
1. Welcome and context setting (5 minutes)
2. Business context review based on pre-work (10–15 minutes)
3. Opportunity discovery: day-in-the-life workflows and bottlenecks (15–20 minutes)
4. Use case prioritization by business impact and effort (15–20 minutes)
5. Next steps and action planning, with owners (5 minutes)

> **Boundary:** Don't attach internal playbooks, private account research, or uncleared peer-customer details. Localize times, ask about accessibility needs, and get recording consent through the proper process.

### Prime the conversation before the meeting
@src S69
Glean's Blueprint training recommends a pre-Visioning email. It tells the prospect the first meeting will focus on use cases and asks them to think in advance about the bottlenecks their teams face. Participants who arrive with real examples make the session far more productive than a blank-canvas brainstorm.

Figure: flow | A 60-minute Visioning agenda | A five-part agenda: welcome and purpose for 5 minutes, context and outcomes for 10, opportunity discovery for 20, prioritization for 20, and commitments for 5.
- Welcome and purpose :: 5 minutes
- Context and outcomes :: 10 minutes
- Opportunity discovery :: 20 minutes
- Prioritization :: 20 minutes
- *Commitments :: 5 minutes; owners and next steps

### Practice
Draft the essential content of a Visioning invitation for a fictional HR operations team.

### Worked response
"**Purpose:** a 60-minute working session to align AI opportunities with your HR operations goals. **Please bring:** your team's goals and KPIs, two recurring workflows that take significant time, the systems involved, and where the friction is. **Pre-work:** please return the attached worksheet two days before the session. **Outcome:** a prioritized list of use cases with owners, and agreed next steps for short workflow interviews. **Roles:** [partner name] facilitates; Glean captures notes and answers technical questions."

## SBP-104-L06 Internal rehearsal and session readiness
@src S07 S05 S02 S26

A short internal rehearsal prevents the most common Visioning failures: unclear roles, a product pitch disguised as discovery, and broken tools.

### What to align on
@src S07
@quote S07 | Reviews worksheet input, prior meeting notes, and aligns on session objectives and preparation with AE.
The AE, SE, partner, and any value specialist should agree on:
- The value hypothesis and what is known, assumed, and missing.
- The agenda and who leads each segment.
- Example use cases to offer if the customer is stuck.
- The tools: test the shared worksheet or whiteboard, and agree a fallback note-taking method.
- The expected outputs and the next decision.

### Rehearse three moments
1. **The opening:** purpose, outcomes, and a collaborative tone.
2. **One diagnostic question:** for example, "Walk me through the last time this took longer than it should have."
3. **The transition to prioritization:** how you will move from brainstorm to ranking.

Pre-seed plausible use cases from approved patterns, but mark them as hypotheses. The customer should feel heard, not steered into a product pitch.

> **Note on timing:** Durations differ across Glean playbooks. This course uses a 60-minute working agenda. If you get less time, cut scope deliberately. For example, prioritize one department rather than pretending everything still fits.

### At a glance
Rehearse the three moments that most often go wrong. They are the ones that set the tone, test your discovery, and secure the outcome.

Figure: flow | Rehearse three moments | Three moments to rehearse before Visioning: the opening, which states purpose and the decision; one diagnostic question, asked about a real recent example; and the transition to prioritization, which moves from ideas to choices.
- The opening :: Purpose, agenda, and the decision needed
- A diagnostic question :: About a real, recent example
- *The transition :: From ideas to ranked choices

### Practice
Run a five-minute readiness review for tomorrow's Visioning session. List what you check, and name one reason that would justify delaying.

### Worked response
Check: the sponsor path, the returned worksheet, the attendee list against the lines of business, the hypotheses, the roles, the tools and fallback, and the expected outputs.

Delay if no one can confirm the business outcome the session supports, or if the workflow owners can't attend. Without them, the use case list will have no owners and no credible path to DITL.
