## SBP-112-L02 Ground prompts in sources and schemas
@src S25 S37 S13

AI output is only as good as its prompt. A grounded prompt specifies what the model may use, what it must produce, and what it must not invent.

### The parts of a grounded prompt
@src S25
@quote S25 | Using only that transcript, provide a clear, concise synopsis of the call.
@quote S25 | Do not add recommendations, new questions, or coaching — only describe what happened on the call.
Glean's use-case scoping prep template shows the pattern. It states the source ("Using only that transcript…"), defines the output structure, and sets limits ("Do not add recommendations, new questions, or coaching"). A grounded prompt contains:
- **Task:** what to produce.
- **Authorized inputs:** which sources the model may use.
- **Known facts and unknowns:** what is already established and what isn't.
- **Output structure:** the table or schema to fill in.
- **Guardrails:** no invented baselines, owners, capabilities, or commitments.
- **Citations:** a reference for each factual claim.
- **Separation:** customer statements, inferences, and benchmark examples are labeled differently.

### Check the citation, not just its presence
A citation can be irrelevant or out of date. Read the passage it points to and check access rights. Instructions that appear inside retrieved documents are data, not new instructions for the model.

### At a glance
A grounded prompt states the task and also what the model may use and what it must not invent.

Figure: stack | The parts of a grounded prompt | Seven stacked parts of a grounded prompt: the task, authorized inputs, known facts and unknowns, the output structure, guardrails, citations, and separating facts from estimates.
- Task :: What to produce, for whom
- Authorized inputs :: Only approved sources
- Facts and unknowns :: State both explicitly
- Output structure :: A schema or template
- Guardrails :: What must not be invented
- Citations :: Every claim points to a source
- *Separation :: Facts, estimates, and assumptions kept apart

### Practice
Improve this prompt: "Make a convincing ROI case."

### Worked response
"Using only the attached workflow notes and metric cards, draft a value model. Include the current and future workflow, each assumption labeled with its evidence and source, explicit units, and conservative, base, and upside ranges. List any missing evidence and the questions needed to validate it. Don't invent customer facts, baselines, or capabilities. Mark this output as a draft requiring financial and technical review."

## SBP-112-L03 Draft value hypotheses without copying outcomes
@src S37 S36 S13

AI drafting tools sometimes pull in other customers' outcomes. A peer's result is not evidence about the prospect.

### Build from the customer's own evidence
@src S36 S13
@quote S13 | Without clear evidence standards, teams either present benchmarks as proof or fail to tell strong stories with real data.
Use the customer's strategy, meeting notes, and stated priorities to draft strategic priorities, risks, and proposed Positive Business Outcomes. A safe draft is labeled "hypothesis to validate" and links to the customer evidence it is based on. Glean's Value Playbook warns against the failure where teams present benchmarks as proof.

### Peer examples are analogies at most
A peer example can be used as an analogy only when it is approved for that use. Keep a possible support-efficiency opportunity for this prospect separate from a proven outcome at another company.

> **Boundary:** Never turn another customer's success into the prospect's own result. Remove uncleared names, figures, and confidential details before sharing with partners or customers.

### At a glance
Another customer's result is an analogy at most. The hypothesis has to be built from this customer's evidence.

Figure: compare | Copied outcome versus grounded hypothesis | A comparison of a copied outcome, which reuses a peer's result as a promise, with a grounded hypothesis, which uses the customer's own volumes and times, states assumptions, and labels evidence.
- Copied outcome || Grounded hypothesis
- Reuses a peer's percentage || Uses this customer's volumes and times
- Implies a promise || States assumptions and a range
- No owner for the claim || Labels evidence and names an owner

### Practice
Repair this AI-drafted sentence: "This customer saved 40% because a peer did."

### Worked response
Replace it with a hypothesis tied to the prospect's own workflow, for example: "Hypothesis to validate: agents' search time for access cases could fall, based on what the team described in Visioning. We'll measure handle time against a four-week baseline."

Remove the peer's percentage and name. Use a peer example only if it is approved, and label it clearly as an external example.

## SBP-112-L04 AI-assisted DITL timeline drafting
@src S38 S22 S02

AI can produce a tidy DITL timeline in seconds. Treat it as a discussion starter until the customer's real workflow evidence replaces the generic content.

### Draft from evidence
@src S38
An AI draft can organize as-is and to-be steps, durations, applications, and possible Glean assistance. Give it real interview evidence, and ask it to:
- Keep current and future timelines separate.
- Include the actor, system, active time, waiting time, evidence, handoff, control, and exception for each step.
- Mark any unknown as "unknown." Never fill gaps with estimates.

### Review every step
A model working from general role knowledge may produce a plausible workflow, but it isn't the customer's observed process. Replace generic steps with steps confirmed in the interviews, and keep the revisions traceable. The SE reviews each proposed capability, and the workflow owner validates the sequence and timings.

> **Boundary:** Don't accept generated timings as baseline data. Don't remove approval or security controls because a model suggests automating them.

### A practical drafting pattern
@src S64
Glean's DITL training describes a practical pattern for a seller without an SE on the call: give Glean the department and the current steps, and ask for a step-by-step optimized workflow. That gives you a starting draft for the future state. It is a draft, not evidence. The SE verifies each capability, and the workflow owner confirms the sequence and timings.

Figure: flow | AI-assisted DITL drafting | A four-step pattern: provide the department and current steps from the interview, ask AI for a step-by-step future-state draft, have the SE verify each capability, and have the workflow owner confirm the sequence and timings.
- Provide the evidence :: Department and current steps
- Ask for a draft :: A step-by-step future state
- SE verifies :: Each proposed capability
- *Owner confirms :: Sequence, ranges, and exceptions

### Practice
You've been handed a generated DITL timeline with precise times for each step, but no citations. Review it.

### Worked response
Mark every time as unverified. Seek interview or observation evidence for each one, and check the step sequence and systems with the workflow owner. Review the future-state claims with the technical owners. Only then use the timeline in a value model.

## SBP-112-L05 Verify, protect and approve AI output
@src S37 S15 S13 S55

People remain accountable for anything that goes to a customer, and for every decision. Review AI output systematically before you use it.

### The review checklist
- **Factual support:** does the cited passage actually support the claim?
- **Freshness:** is the source current?
- **Permissions:** may this content be shared with this audience?
- **Confidentiality:** does it contain customer or internal information that must not be shared?
- **Technical feasibility:** has the SE verified each capability?
- **Arithmetic and units:** are the calculations correct?
- **Bias and framing:** are downside results kept?
- **Claims and clearance:** are external claims approved?

### Classify content before publishing
A draft can pass citation review and still fail, for example because it includes internal pilot thresholds or restricted customer details. Classify each section as learner-safe, author-only, or pending clearance.

> **Boundary:** Never give AI authority to submit pilot approvals, send customer messages, change systems, or produce an SME sign-off. Consequential actions go through authorized human processes.

### At a glance
Polish isn't proof. Run every AI-assisted output through the same checks before anyone outside the team sees it.

Figure: cards | Review checks for AI output | Eight cards listing review checks for AI-assisted output: factual support, freshness, permissions, confidentiality, technical feasibility, arithmetic and units, bias and framing, and claims clearance.
- Factual support :: Every claim traced
- Freshness :: Sources still current
- Permissions :: Allowed to use this input
- Confidentiality :: Nothing restricted exposed
- Feasibility :: Verified by a technical owner
- Arithmetic and units :: Recalculated
- Bias and framing :: Fair to the evidence
- *Claims clearance :: Approved before sharing

### Practice
Give three reasons to reject a polished, AI-generated executive deck.

### Worked response
1. **Unsupported customer metrics:** figures with no customer source or baseline.
2. **Unverified product commitments:** capabilities that no SE has confirmed for this environment.
3. **Internal or restricted content:** for example, internal pilot policy or another customer's details.

Correct each one, recheck the source passages, and get the appropriate review before sharing.

## SBP-112-L06 Build a reusable human-reviewed workflow
@src S25 S37 S38 S55

Make AI preparation repeatable: a set sequence of steps, with human review at each gate and a safe stop when something is missing.

### A reusable preparation flow
1. **Collect** the permitted sources, and record their versions.
2. **Extract** claims, each with its source passage.
3. **Draft** structured outputs: hypothesis, use-case log, DITL tables, and value model.
4. **List gaps** and open questions.
5. **Check** the calculations and units.
6. **Route reviews** to the AE, the SE, and customer owners.
7. **Log** the approval status before anything is published.

This course's prompt library has separate prompts for research, hypotheses, discovery, DITL, value QA, and readiness review. Together they produce a draft artifact pack and a list of open questions, not an approved Blueprint.

### Fail safely
If an internal tool isn't available, use the equivalent structured prompt in an approved environment. If required evidence or reviewers are missing, stop. Don't proceed with gaps.

### At a glance
A reusable workflow puts the human review steps in the process, so they aren't skipped under time pressure.

Figure: cycle | A reusable, human-reviewed workflow | A seven-step loop: collect authorized inputs, extract facts, draft, list gaps, check, route reviews, and log decisions, then repeat for the next account.
- Collect :: Authorized inputs only
- Extract :: Facts with sources
- Draft :: Structured output
- List gaps :: What's still unknown
- Check :: Against the review list
- *Route reviews :: To the right owners
- Log :: Decisions and changes

### Practice
Design a stop condition for an AI-assisted build pipeline.

### Worked response
"Stop external publication if any required source is missing, any customer claim is unsupported, any capability is unverified, any clearance is pending, or any review is incomplete. Produce an exception report listing each item, its owner, and the next action."
