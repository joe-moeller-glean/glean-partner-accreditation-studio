# SBP-103 Roles, Partners and Engagement Models

## SBP-103-L01 Roles across the three steps
@src S02 S03 S05 S07

A Blueprint works when everyone knows their job before the customer joins the call. This lesson describes the usual division of labor and how to make it explicit for each engagement.

### Who normally does what
@src S03 S07 S14
@quote S03 | AE should lead this conversation with SE adding technical questions and support
@quote S14 | Enable a joint AE/SE run‑of‑show with decision‑makers present.
- **Account Executive (AE).** Owns account strategy, customer coordination, and sponsor alignment. The AE secures executive buy-in before Visioning, finalizes the stakeholder list, and sends the agenda. Glean's Blueprint template has the AE lead the Visioning conversation, with the SE adding technical questions.
- **Solutions Engineer (SE).** Normally leads technical and workflow depth in Day-in-the-Life (DITL) sessions. The SE validates feasibility and data assumptions and advises on how many DITL sessions the scope needs.
- **Joint AE and SE.** Executive Validation is a joint run-of-show with the decision-makers present.
- **Business Value Consultant (BVC).** Provides value expertise when the engagement warrants it. A BVC can facilitate, review a complex model, or build the executive business case.
- **Partner.** Can facilitate discovery, bring industry and departmental context, and contribute delivery insight to the roadmap.

### Make the run-of-show explicit
Before each customer meeting, agree on these roles:

Table: Run-of-show roles
| Role | Responsibility in the session |
|---|---|
| Lead facilitator | Keeps time, steers toward outcomes, manages participation |
| Note-taker | Captures the use case log, quotes, metrics, and open questions |
| Technical respondent | Answers or parks feasibility questions; never guesses |
| Value lead | Probes for baselines, volumes, and business impact |
| Sponsor owner | Keeps the executive informed and books the decision meeting |
| Follow-up owner | Sends the recap and updates the mutual action plan |

One person can fill more than one role, but no role should be left implicit.

> **Boundary:** These are operating principles, not a universal contractual RACI. Agree the split for each deal, and don't assume a partner can authorize Glean resources or commercial terms.

### The ideal state, and the real one
@src S75
Glean's Blueprint training describes the ideal state as the AE and SE working together on every step, from Visioning through DITL to the executive readout. The training is also candid that SE capacity is sometimes tight, so an AE may run steps an SE ideally would. When that happens, the AE shouldn't answer technical feasibility questions on the spot. Park them for the SE, so the deal doesn't stall and nothing unverified is promised.

For partners, the same principle applies. Decide who owns each role before the session, and note who will answer the questions nobody in the room can.

Figure: cards | Who leads each step | Five cards showing typical ownership: the AE leads Visioning with SE support; the SE leads DITL workflow mapping; the AE and SE jointly lead Executive Validation; the Business Value Consultant supports complex value cases; and the partner assists, co-leads, or leads by agreement.
- Account Executive :: Leads Visioning; owns the sponsor path
- Solutions Engineer :: Leads DITL mapping and feasibility
- *AE and SE jointly :: Lead Executive Validation
- Business Value Consultant :: Supports complex value cases
- Partner :: Assists, co-leads, or leads, by agreement

### Practice
Assign roles for a partner-led Visioning workshop and the DITL sessions that follow it. The team is an AE, an SE, a partner consultant, and a BVC who is available for review only.

### Worked response
- **Visioning:** the partner facilitates and captures the use case log. The AE coordinates the customer and sponsor and owns the commercial context. The SE is the technical respondent.
- **DITL:** the SE leads workflow and technical depth, the partner co-interviews and maps workflows, and the AE keeps the sponsor path moving.
- **Value:** the SE or partner drafts the estimate. The BVC reviews it only if the case is complex and the engagement has been agreed.
- **Follow-up:** the AE sends the recap and updates the mutual action plan within 24 hours.

## SBP-103-L02 Select a partner engagement model
@src S05 S06 S21

Partners can contribute at different depths. Choose the model based on customer relationships, the partner's Glean competence, available capacity, and what the deal needs.

### Three models
@src S05
@quote S05 | Partner owns day-to-day agent builds and optimization, while Glean provides patterns, technical guidance, and product support.
Table: Partner engagement models
| Model | What the partner does | What Glean does | When it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assist | Joins Glean-led sessions, adds industry context, takes notes | Leads facilitation, technical depth, and value | A partner new to Blueprinting, or a strategic deal Glean must lead |
| Co-lead | Facilitates parts of Visioning and DITL, owns stakeholder engagement | Provides technical backup and reviews outputs | A partner with domain expertise but limited Glean experience |
| Partner-led | Leads agreed discovery and follow-on services | Coaches, sets technical boundaries, owns product answers | A partner with demonstrated, reviewed Blueprint competence |

### Progress through evidence
A partner with strong departmental access can accelerate end-user interviews from day one. Build Blueprint competence step by step: observe the first workshop, co-facilitate the second, then lead a third that Glean reviews. This progression is a training recommendation from this course, not a Glean policy.

### What "partner-led" does not mean
Partner-led does not mean bypassing Glean's internal approvals. Before any partner-led session, confirm:
- The customer has agreed to the partner's involvement and to how information will be shared between organizations.
- What the partner may say about product capabilities, implementation scope, and timelines.
- Who answers technical questions the partner can't resolve, and how quickly.

### At a glance
Partners move from assisting to leading by showing evidence, not by declaring readiness.

Figure: flow | Progressing through engagement models | A three-step progression of partner engagement models: assist in Glean-led sessions, co-lead parts of Visioning and DITL, then lead agreed discovery with Glean coaching. Each step requires evidence from the previous one.
- Assist :: Join Glean-led sessions; add industry context
- Co-lead :: Facilitate parts of Visioning and DITL
- *Partner-led :: Lead agreed discovery; Glean coaches and reviews

### Practice
A partner has deep financial-services expertise but has never run a Glean Blueprint. Choose an engagement model and explain how the partner could progress.

### Worked response
Co-lead. The partner handles industry context and stakeholder engagement, and may facilitate the DITL interviews with front-line users. Glean supplies technical backup during sessions and reviews the use case log, workflow maps, and value inputs before anything goes to the executive.

Move to partner-led only after the partner's outputs pass review on at least one engagement, and with the customer's agreement.

## SBP-103-L03 Stakeholder coverage and RACI
@src S05 S07 S14

Blueprint conclusions are only as good as the people who informed them. You need business sponsorship, functional leadership, front-line workflow knowledge, and technical or data ownership. Security and compliance join when they are relevant.

### The voices you need
@src S07 S14
@quote S07 | Finalize relevant stakeholder list (representing all necessary lines of business).
Table: Stakeholder roles in a Blueprint
| Stakeholder | What they contribute | Typical session |
|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Aligns the investment with strategy; makes the decision | Executive Validation |
| Business or functional owner | Defines impact, owns the outcome metric | Visioning, DITL |
| Power user or front-line employee | Explains how the work really happens | DITL |
| IT or data owner | Confirms systems, access, and data quality | DITL, technical follow-up |
| Security or compliance | Sets data-handling and review requirements | Technical and security alignment |
| Procurement | Owns purchasing steps and timing | Paper-process checkpoints |

### Build a stakeholder map
For each person, record which of four things they hold: a **decision**, an **input**, a **metric**, or a **dependency**. Invite the right voices to each meeting, not everyone to every meeting. A DITL session with ten observers and one user produces less evidence than a session with two users and a note-taker.

> **Watch for:** Mistaking seniority for workflow expertise. A director can describe the target state, but often only the people doing the work can describe today's friction accurately. Respect recording, privacy, and access constraints when notes are shared between organizations.

### Who to interview depends on the account
@src S64
Glean's DITL training notes that the right interviewee shifts with account size. In corporate and commercial accounts, the person one level below the VP often understands the workflow well enough to walk through it step by step. In enterprise and strategic accounts, you usually need the people who do the work every day, the employees whose work would actually change.

Either way, ask the question directly: "Who touches this workflow, and who would be using the new process?"

Figure: matrix | Stakeholder coverage by influence and workflow knowledge | A two-by-two matrix with workflow knowledge on the horizontal axis and decision influence on the vertical axis. High influence and high knowledge: business owners, who define impact. High influence and low knowledge: the executive sponsor, who makes the decision. Low influence and high knowledge: front-line users, who explain the real work. Low influence and low knowledge: inform as needed.
- x: Workflow knowledge (low to high)
- y: Decision influence
- tl: Executive sponsor :: Aligns investment and makes the decision
- tr: Business owner :: Defines impact and owns the metric
- bl: Inform as needed :: Procurement, wider stakeholders
- br: Front-line users :: Explain how the work really happens

### Practice
A DITL interview for a fictional HR service-desk use case is attended only by the AE and an IT administrator. What is missing, and who would you add?

### Worked response
Missing: ownership of the business outcome and metric, and evidence of the front-line workflow. The IT administrator can confirm systems and access but can't speak for how HR agents handle cases or which measures matter.

Add the HR service-delivery lead (outcome and metric owner) and two or three representative HR agents (workflow). Keep the IT administrator for systems and data questions, and maintain the path to the executive sponsor through the AE.

## SBP-103-L04 Business Value Consulting engagement
@src S13 S28

Not every value case needs a specialist. Match the depth of value support to the opportunity's complexity, decision timing, data availability, and what the sponsor needs.

### Value support comes in levels
@src S13
@quote S13 | framework to match value work intensity to deal characteristics.
Glean's Value Playbook sets out tiered engagement levels that match the intensity of value work to the deal. At the light end, AE and SE teams use playbook materials, standard talking points, and the self-serve calculator. At the deeper end, a Business Value Consultant builds a customer-specific hypothesis, runs discovery, models value, and produces an executive business case.

### What a BVC engagement looks like
@src S28
@quote S28 | Take the AE/SE intake; qualify it against the tiering model
Glean's BVC enablement describes a value engagement as a sequence: triage and qualify the request, frame the hypothesis, run discovery, pull the evidence, build the model, build and quality-check the readout, then present it and track realization. A BVC engagement begins with an AE and SE intake request. It is qualified against the tiering model before scope, deliverables, and dates are agreed.

### Choosing the right level
- **Self-serve** is enough for a straightforward, directional estimate on one or two use cases with available inputs.
- **Expert review** is worth requesting for multi-department attribution, complex cost or revenue models, or a high-stakes executive business case.

> **Boundary:** Don't promise customers a dedicated value consultant. Engagement thresholds and capacity are internal routing details, not customer entitlements. Use the live intake path and agree ownership and timing before committing externally.

### Match value effort to the deal
@src S13
@quote S13 | A full business case takes 2-3 weeks.
Glean's Value Playbook gives a practical timing check: a full business case takes two to three weeks. If the decision is next week, go lightweight. The playbook also asks whether there is at least one workflow with a measurable before and after. If the value is purely qualitative, a business case won't strengthen the deal.

Figure: stack | Levels of value support | Three stacked levels of value support, from lightest to heaviest: self-serve estimates using the approved calculator, expert review of an account team's model, and a full Business Value Consulting engagement for complex, high-stakes cases.
- Self-serve :: Account team builds the estimate with approved tools
- Expert review :: A value specialist checks the model and story
- *Full BVC engagement :: Complex, high-stakes cases; about two to three weeks

### Practice
Choose value support for two situations: a simple two-use-case estimate for one support team, and a multi-business-unit case that the CFO will review.

### Worked response
- **Simple case:** use the standard calculator, with the AE and SE reviewing the assumptions and evidence labels.
- **Multi-business-unit case:** submit a BVC intake request for scoping and review. Agree who owns the model and the timing before telling the customer when the business case will be ready.

## SBP-103-L05 Separate discovery, build and support
@src S05 S43 S44

Discovery often uncovers work that doesn't belong in discovery. Keep three kinds of work distinct: discovering use cases, building solutions, and supporting the platform.

### The lifecycle split
@src S05 S43
@quote S43 | Partners own outcome delivery, rollout enablement, and change management.
Glean's partner model distinguishes three kinds of work:
- **Discovery:** the partner facilitates workshops with business stakeholders, and Glean shares patterns and technical constraints.
- **Build:** the partner leads day-to-day agent building with Glean coaching and patterns.
- **Optimize and support:** the partner handles front-line advisory work and small changes, and Glean Support handles product issues.

Glean's partner-led services portfolio lists the kinds of work partners deliver, including custom connector builds, agent building and governance, end-user training, transformation consulting and change management, support and managed services, and PMO and business reviews.

### Where a discovery finding goes
A Blueprint finding can become an agent-build service, an adoption and training program, or a knowledge-readiness project. A suspected product defect stays a Glean product-support matter. The partner can gather safe diagnostic evidence, but it does not promise to fix the platform.

### Scope before delivery
@src S44
@quote S44 | must be documented in a written amendment or change order signed by the applicable parties.
A discovery scope is not a signed delivery statement of work (SOW). Glean's partner SOW template keeps out of scope anything beyond the package boundary, such as unsupported connectors or work that needs Glean Engineering development. It requires a written amendment or change order for any change to scope. Turn assumptions, exclusions, and dependencies into an explicit scope before delivery begins.

### At a glance
A discovery finding is a candidate, not a commitment. Route each one to the phase that owns it.

Figure: flow | Where a discovery finding goes | A three-step lifecycle split. Discovery captures and prioritizes the finding. Build scopes and delivers it under an agreed statement of work. Optimize and support improves it after go-live.
- Discovery :: Capture, rank, and assign an owner
- Build :: Scope and deliver under an agreed SOW
- *Optimize and support :: Improve and measure after go-live

### Practice
After a DITL session, the customer asks for three things: a new agent for policy questions, user training for 200 HR agents, and help with search results that seem to ignore a permission change. Route each one.

### Worked response
- **New agent:** a scoped partner delivery with Glean technical guidance, under an agreed SOW or change order.
- **Training:** whoever owns enablement in the agreed plan. This is often a partner training service.
- **Permission issue:** a suspected product or configuration issue that goes to Glean Support. The partner gathers safe diagnostic evidence, such as the query, the expected and actual result, and the timing, without sharing confidential content more widely than needed.

## SBP-103-L06 Joint operating agreement and escalation
@src S05 S16 S44

When two organizations work one account, ambiguity costs time and credibility. A short operating agreement before delivery prevents most problems.

### What to agree up front
- **Customer communications:** who sends what, and who is copied.
- **Artifact storage:** where the use case log, workflow maps, and value model live, and who can see them.
- **Technical answers:** who answers feasibility and product questions, and how unresolved ones are tracked.
- **Value review:** who checks assumptions and evidence labels before anything reaches the customer.
- **Approvals:** which decisions need Glean's internal approval, such as pilots, pricing, and roadmap statements.
- **Follow-up:** who owns the recap and the mutual action plan after each session.

### Disagreements and unverified claims
Keep a decision log. When the team disagrees, or a product or policy question is unresolved, escalate it. Don't guess in front of the customer. One accountable owner per decision is better than shared ambiguity.

> **Example:** A partner believes a custom data source is supported, but the SE hasn't verified it. Log feasibility as unresolved, assign technical validation, and keep the first-wave scope conditional until the answer is documented.

### At a glance
Agree the operating rules before the first customer session, not during an escalation.

Figure: cards | Joint operating agreement | Six cards listing what Glean and the partner agree up front: who sends customer communications, where artifacts are stored, who answers technical questions, who reviews value claims, who approves commitments, and who owns follow-up.
- Customer communications :: Who sends what, and when
- Artifact storage :: One agreed, access-controlled location
- *Technical answers :: Only verified answers; park the rest
- Value review :: Who checks numbers before they are shared
- Approvals :: Who can commit to scope or terms
- Follow-up :: Who sends the recap and updates the plan

### Practice
A partner consultant wants to tell the customer that a write-back integration to their ticketing system "will be in the first release." The SE hasn't verified it. Resolve the disagreement.

### Worked response
Pause the commitment. Describe the integration as an unverified dependency in the decision log. Assign product and SE validation, including security review, if write-back changes data in a customer system. Leave it out of the roadmap and value case until the result is documented.

If the customer asks in the meantime, say: "We're validating that integration and will confirm by the date in our action plan."
