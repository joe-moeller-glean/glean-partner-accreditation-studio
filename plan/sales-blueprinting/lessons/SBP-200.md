# SBP-200 Blueprint Capstone Simulation

## SBP-200-L01 Read the fictional scenario and evidence rules
@src S02 S13 S54

The capstone puts the whole Blueprint method into practice on one realistic case. Before you start, understand what the scenario gives you, what it deliberately leaves out, and how evidence will be judged.

### The organization
The case is an unnamed, fictional knowledge-work organization with 2,000 employees. It is not a real customer, and no external research is needed.
- **The COO** is the simulated executive sponsor and wants more support capacity this quarter, without losing quality.
- **The CRO** wants more consistent meeting preparation in sales.
- **Support Operations** owns the first metric.
- **A technical champion** coordinates the platform owners.
- **The procurement and signature process** is unknown.

### Three kinds of input
Table: How to treat scenario inputs
| Kind | Example from the case | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Scenario fact | 200 potential support users; 240 workdays a year | A training fact, labeled as such |
| Estimate | Interview timings, such as eight minutes to find approved guidance | An estimate, not a measured baseline |
| Deliberate unknown | Connector and permission behavior; procurement dates; the baseline | An open question with an owner and a validation step |

### Evidence rules
@src S13
@quote S13 | Every ROI estimate must carry its assumptions, risk adjustments, and evidence tier transparently.
Label every figure you use. Any assumption you add yourself must be recorded with an owner and a validation plan. The assessor answers questions using only the scenario's facts and will say "unknown, must validate" for anything the scenario doesn't contain.

> **Boundary:** Don't invent a customer name, a real case study, an approved product capability, or a measured outcome. Real customer stories and internal images are not required, and shouldn't be used.

### At a glance
Every input in the scenario has a status. Use it exactly as labeled.

Figure: cards | Three kinds of scenario input | Three cards naming the kinds of input in the capstone scenario: facts, which you may use as stated; estimates, which you must show as ranges with assumptions; and unknowns, which you must turn into open questions with owners.
- Facts :: Use as stated
- Estimates :: Show as ranges, with assumptions
- *Unknowns :: Open questions, each with an owner

### Practice
Annotate three scenario items as fact, estimate, or unknown: the 200 support users, the eight-minute search time, and connector permission behavior.

### Worked response
- **200 support users:** a scenario fact for training purposes. It is the addressable population, not the adopted population.
- **Eight-minute search time:** an interview estimate of active work, not a measured baseline.
- **Connector permission behavior:** unknown. Record it as an open technical question for the technical owner, with a validation step.

## SBP-200-L02 Prepare and run the Visioning simulation
@src S05 S07 S06

The first deliverable shows that you can prepare for and facilitate a Visioning conversation that leads to a decision, not just a brainstorm.

### What you submit
- **A value hypothesis:** the Three Whys and proposed Positive Business Outcomes, built from the scenario.
- **A stakeholder map:** the sponsor, champion, metric and data owner, technical owners, and the procurement gap.
- **A readiness review:** the go, narrow, or wait judgment, with its rationale.
- **A timed agenda.**
- **A use-case log and an impact-and-effort matrix**, with your first-wave picks and the reasons for deferring the rest.
- **A five-minute facilitation segment**, live or recorded.

### The role-play
@src S07
@quote S07 | Set an open, consultative, and strategic tone—avoid checklist-style or interrogative discovery.
Role cards give each participant a stance. The sponsor asks about business impact. A front-line user describes the current work. The technical owner raises permission and connector constraints.

Distracting requests will also come up: autonomous refund approval, perfect cross-system BI joins, and a custom agent for a legacy source. Park or phase each one based on effort and risk, and ask diagnostic questions. Don't claim that they are supported or unsupported.

### What earns full credit
Outcome-led, non-leading facilitation that captures evidence, shows how the evidence changed your hypothesis, and ends with an explicit, simulated prioritization decision. A polished deck alone isn't enough.

### At a glance
The Visioning submission has six parts. The facilitation segment is where assessors see the method in action.

Figure: flow | Visioning simulation submission | Six parts of the Visioning submission in order: value hypothesis, stakeholder map, readiness review, timed agenda, use case log and matrix, and a five-minute facilitation segment.
- Value hypothesis :: Why change, why now, why Glean
- Stakeholder map :: Who decides and who knows
- Readiness review :: Go, narrow, or reschedule
- Timed agenda :: 60 minutes
- Use case log and matrix :: Ranked with reasons
- *Facilitation segment :: Five minutes, role-played

### Practice
The sponsor's first comment is, "Can your AI just approve refunds automatically?" Respond in role.

### Worked response
"That's a useful idea to capture. Before deciding whether it belongs in the first wave, can we understand how refunds are approved today, who's accountable, and what controls apply? We'll note it as a candidate with its authorization questions, and come back to it once we've looked at the higher-frequency support work you mentioned."

Record it as a parked candidate with high effort and risk, and its open questions.

## SBP-200-L03 Document current and future workflows
@src S22 S02 S13

The second deliverable is two detailed current-and-future workflow pairs, showing exactly where time goes and what a change would and would not alter.

### The two workflows
**Support, approved-guidance discovery.** Interview estimates of active work:
- Read the case: 2 minutes.
- Find approved guidance: 8 minutes.
- Consult a colleague: 3 minutes.
- Draft a response: 5 minutes.
- Review and send: 2 minutes.

That makes 20 active minutes in total. Some cases also wait one business day for specialist input. Keep that as elapsed time, not labor.

**Sales, customer-meeting preparation.** Reviewing CRM takes 10 minutes, finding notes 10 minutes, locating relevant content 15 minutes, and drafting a brief 10 minutes. Frequency and comparison data are unknown.

### What the maps must show
- The actor, step, and system for each step.
- Active time and elapsed delay, kept separate.
- Handoffs and failure points.
- Evidence labels for every timing.
- In the future state: retained human controls (source review, final approval, escalation), data and permission dependencies, and the hypothesis label on every future timing.

Submit a playback correction log showing at least one change made after feedback from the workflow owner.

> **Boundary:** Model only the avoidable knowledge-friction burden, the eight-minute search step, in the value case, not all 20 minutes. Don't remove reading or review controls, and don't monetize the one-day wait as labor saved.

### At a glance
The future state changes some steps and keeps others. Human review and send stay with the agent.

### Practice
A teammate's future-state support map removes the review-and-send step "because AI drafts the response." Correct it.

### Worked response
Restore the review-and-send step. The human agent keeps source review and final approval of the response, and escalation remains.

The future-state hypothesis is that grounded access to approved guidance may reduce the search step, and might support drafting. The exact timing is unknown until validated. List the dependencies: access to the approved knowledge repository, and correct permission behavior.

## SBP-200-L04 Build and defend the value case
@src S13 S39

The value case is worth the most points on the rubric. It must be reproducible, honest about uncertainty, and clear about what kind of value it shows.

### The base assumptions
Table: Base modeling assumptions (fictional training inputs)
| Input | Base value |
|---|---|
| Addressable support users | 200 |
| Adoption | 50% |
| Events per active user per workday | 2 |
| Workdays per year | 240 |
| Avoidable burden per event | 8 minutes |
| Removable fraction | 60% |
| Relevant coverage | 80% |
| Recapture | 25% |
| Loaded rate | $60 per hour (capacity value) |
| Risk adjustment | 10% |
| First-year all-in cost | $30,000 |

These numbers illustrate explicit modeling. They aren't Glean's approved defaults, pricing, or actual results. The ROI Lab reproduces them: risk-adjusted capacity value of $41,472 in the base case, against $30,000 of cost.

### What to explain
- **Units:** convert minutes to hours once, and annualize explicitly.
- **Adoption versus population**, and coverage versus removable fraction. Confirm the eight minutes is a raw burden, not an already-measured saving, so that you don't discount it twice.
- **Recapture** tied to the support manager's proposal to redeploy capacity to backlog reduction. No headcount reduction or avoided hire has been approved, so the benefit is directional capacity, not cash.
- **Conservative, base, and upside** scenarios. The conservative case is below cost, and it stays visible.
- **Sensitivity:** which inputs drive the range, and how you will validate them.
- **Metric cards:** the cohort, a baseline plan (no timing sample exists yet), the owner, and a quality guardrail.

> **Boundary:** Don't add a sales benefit to the support total until sales frequency, adoption, and overlap are validated. Don't guarantee any financial outcome.

### At a glance
The defense matters as much as the number. Expect questions on each of these six points.

Figure: cards | What the value defense must explain | Six cards naming what the capstone value defense must explain: units, adoption versus population, recapture, the three scenarios, sensitivity, and metric cards.
- Units :: Minutes, hours, and days converted once
- Adoption versus population :: Not everyone uses it
- Recapture :: Saved time isn't all redeployed
- *Three scenarios :: Conservative, base, and upside
- Sensitivity :: Which input matters most
- Metric cards :: How each claim will be measured

### Practice
Explain why the conservative scenario should stay in the executive readout even though it shows a negative return.

### Worked response
It shows the sponsor what would have to be true for the investment not to pay back, for example low adoption and low recapture. That points directly to what the validation must test.

Removing it would overstate confidence and undermine credibility with finance. The recommendation should explain how the validation and reallocation plan reduce that downside risk.

## SBP-200-L05 Executive readout, validation recommendation and MAP
@src S14 S15 S45 S44

The readout is where you bring the case together into a decision. You'll be challenged, and how you answer matters more than polish.

### What you submit
- **An 8- to 10-minute executive readout**, live or recorded: problem → workflow change → value range and evidence → success criteria → decision and commitments.
- **A validation recommendation:** the lightest option that resolves the remaining buying question, the alternatives you considered, and a stop rule.
- **A mutual action plan** with owners, dates, and dependencies, distinguishing agreed dates from proposed ones.
- **A recap and a delivery and value handoff**, including outstanding risks.

### The sponsor's three questions
@src S14
@quote S14 | Decide validation path (Glean Lab by default; Pilot/PoC only if necessary)
The simulated sponsor will ask:
1. "Why not just give us a pilot?"
2. "How does saved time turn into a business result?"
3. "What evidence will replace these estimates?"

Answer honestly. Clarify the unresolved buying question, and defend a scoped alternative where it fits. If a pilot really is needed, state the readiness and internal approval requirements.

> **Boundary:** The simulation does not grant approval to deploy, send communications, or create CRM records. Draft artifacts stay in your submission. Presenting a role-play decision or a readiness score as real pilot approval fails a critical gate.

### At a glance
The readout ends with a decision, a validation recommendation, and a plan. The sponsor will test all three.

Figure: flow | From readout to handoff | A four-step flow for the capstone's final deliverables: an 8- to 10-minute executive readout, a validation recommendation, a mutual action plan, and a recap with the delivery and value handoff.
- Executive readout :: 8 to 10 minutes
- *Validation recommendation :: The lightest sufficient option
- Mutual action plan :: Owners, dates, evidence
- Recap and handoff :: To delivery and value owners

### Practice
Answer the sponsor's first question: "Why not just give us a pilot?"

### Worked response
"We may get there. First, let's be clear about what you need to see to decide. Your team has accepted the support workflow and the directional value. The open question is whether agents will find and trust approved guidance in that flow. A focused Lab scenario, or a tailored demo built around five representative case types, could answer that within weeks.

If a question remains that only your own systems and permissions can answer, we'll scope a pilot properly, with criteria, a baseline, and owners. It would go through Glean's approval process, and nothing is committed until that's complete."

## SBP-200-L06 Self-audit, assessor review and remediation
@src S54 S55 S56

The capstone is assessed by a qualified person using a published rubric and critical gates. Audit your own work against them before you submit.

### The rubric (proposed)
Table: Capstone rubric dimensions
| Dimension | Points |
|---|---|
| Preparation and value hypothesis | 10 |
| Visioning facilitation | 10 |
| Use-case inventory and prioritization | 15 |
| Current-state workflow evidence | 10 |
| Future-state feasibility and controls | 10 |
| Business value model and uncertainty | 20 |
| Executive decision story | 10 |
| Validation path and MAP | 10 |
| Handoff, sources, and responsible AI | 5 |

### The critical gates
Each gate must pass regardless of the score:
- **G01:** no fabricated customer facts, results, or sources.
- **G02:** safe data and access handling.
- **G03:** no unauthorized deployment or commercial commitment.
- **G04:** complete core evidence: the prioritized log, two workflow pairs, the value model, the metric plan, the readout, and the MAP.
- **G05:** material financial and technical integrity, with no uncorrected unit, formula, double-counting, or capability errors.

The proposed pass rule is at least 80 out of 100, with every critical gate passed and every dimension scored. That threshold still needs program-owner approval before launch.

### Review and remediation
A qualified human assessor scores the evidence and records feedback. A second reviewer calibrates borderline scores. If you fall short, fix the specific weakness and resubmit a versioned pack. Assessors keep comments, the score history, and an appeal route. AI can help with review, but it cannot be the sole authority for issuing a credential.

### At a glance
A critical gate failure means remediation, whatever the overall score.

Figure: flow | Review and remediation | A four-step review path: self-audit against the rubric, assessor review, a critical-gate check, then either pass or remediate and resubmit. A failed critical gate requires remediation regardless of the score.
- Self-audit :: Against the rubric
- Assessor review :: Scored with evidence
- *Critical-gate check :: Any failure means remediation
- Pass or remediate :: Resubmit with fixes

### Practice
Your capstone scores 86, but it includes a screenshot that exposes restricted data. What is the outcome?

### Worked response
Not passed. Critical gate G02 has failed, and a high score can't compensate for it. Remove the sensitive data, follow the approved remediation, document the correction, and resubmit for the safe-handling review and reassessment of the affected sections.
