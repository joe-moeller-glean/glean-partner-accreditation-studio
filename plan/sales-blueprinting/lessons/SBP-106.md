# SBP-106 Discover and Prioritize Use Cases

## SBP-106-L01 Use the discovery chain
@src S05 S13

Discovery runs in one direction: from what the business needs to how you would measure it. Starting from a solution, such as "let's build an agent," skips the facts that decide whether it will work.

### The chain
@src S05
@quote S05 | A simple discovery framework: business outcomes → use cases → workflows → data → success metrics.
1. **Business outcome:** what changes for the business, and who owns that result.
2. **Use case:** the actor and the job they need to do.
3. **Workflow:** how the job is done today, with its steps, handoffs, and systems.
4. **Data:** the information the job needs, where it lives, and who can access it.
5. **Success metrics:** the evidence that the outcome improved.

### A worked chain (fictional support team)
Table: Discovery chain for faster case resolution
| Link | Example |
|---|---|
| Outcome | Higher support throughput without lower quality |
| Use case | A support agent finds the approved answer for a case |
| Workflow | Review case → search sources → check with a peer → draft → respond |
| Data | Case context, knowledge base articles, past resolutions |
| Metric | Handle time, paired with resolution quality checks |

### Why order matters
If you start with a complex agent design, you lock in assumptions before you know the data or the workflow. Following the chain exposes missing facts and dependencies, such as an unowned knowledge base or a permission model nobody has checked, while the design can still change. An agent idea is one possible implementation. It is never the starting requirement.

### At a glance
Work the chain from the top. Starting at connectors or agent ideas produces technology in search of a problem.

Figure: flow | The discovery chain | A five-link chain worked from top to bottom: business outcome, use case, workflow, data, and success metrics. Each link is discovered in that order.
- Business outcome :: Higher support throughput, same quality
- Use case :: An agent finds the approved answer
- Workflow :: Review, search, check with a peer, draft
- Data :: Case context, articles, past resolutions
- *Success metrics :: Handle time, with quality checks

### Practice
Put these in discovery order: connectors, outcome, metrics, agent idea, workflow. Explain where the agent idea belongs.

### Worked response
Outcome → a specific use case → workflow evidence → data and connector feasibility → metrics.

The agent idea comes last, as a possible implementation once you understand the work and the data. It is not the starting requirement, and it might not be the right answer at all.

## SBP-106-L02 Ask for diagnostic evidence
@src S05 S07 S13

Good questions produce evidence: real examples, frequency, the people affected, and consequences. Weak questions produce opinions.

### Ask about a real, recent example
@src S05 S07
@quote S05 | "Day in the life" of key personas; pain points, failure modes, and current workarounds; what "good" looks like 6–12 months out.
"Walk me through the last time this happened" gets far more useful detail than "Is search painful?" Follow up with:
- Where did the information live?
- Who else was involved?
- How did you decide the answer was trustworthy?
- How often does this happen, and how long does it take?
- What happens when it goes wrong?

Glean's partner question bank covers four areas: strategic goals and current success metrics, the day in the life of key personas, pain points and workarounds, and what "good" looks like 6 to 12 months out.

### Four questions for every use case
@src S13
@quote S13 | For every use case, answer these four questions
@quote S13 | Volume × per-event impact = total value. Low volume kills ROI.
Glean's Value Playbook answers four questions for every use case:
- **How does it happen today?** Walk through the current workflow step by step to find the friction and the systems involved.
- **How often?** How many times per day, week, or month, and across how many people? Volume multiplied by per-event impact gives total value, and low volume kills ROI.
- **What does it cost today?** The time, money, or risk cost of the current approach. This sets the baseline for a before-and-after comparison.
- **What does good look like?** What success would look like, and which metric would move.

Then ask where the proof would come from: telemetry, surveys, or a system of record.

> **Boundary:** Don't ask customers to guess the ROI before you understand their process. Avoid leading the witness, and keep disagreements in the notes rather than smoothing them over.

### At a glance
Four questions, asked about a real recent example, give you the evidence for impact and effort.

Figure: cards | Four diagnostic questions | Four cards with the diagnostic questions to ask for every use case: how it happens today, how often, what it costs today, and what good looks like.
- How does it happen today? :: Walk through the last real example
- How often? :: Volume and frequency, with a source
- What does it cost today? :: Time, delay, errors, and risk
- *What does good look like? :: The outcome and how you'd measure it

### Practice
Write three questions for a support workflow that would reveal both its impact and its effort.

### Worked response
1. "Walk me through what happens from when a case arrives to when you send the answer. Where do you search, and what access do you need?"
2. "How often does this happen each day, how long does it take, and how many agents do it?"
3. "Who tracks how quickly and how well cases are resolved, and in which system?"

The first exposes effort, meaning the systems and permissions involved. The second exposes reach and volume. The third finds the metric owner and the source of proof.

## SBP-106-L03 Impact, reach and value rationale
@src S02 S05 S13 S21

Impact is more than how many people a use case touches. It combines reach, frequency, and the business consequence: cost, revenue, risk, or quality.

### The parts of impact
@src S05 S13
@quote S05 | Impact: How much positive change this use case creates for the business and users.
- **Reach:** the affected users.
- **Frequency:** how often the event occurs.
- **Consequence:** what happens to cost, revenue, risk, or quality when it goes well or badly.

A workflow used by 200 people twice a day may be a bigger capacity opportunity than a weekly task for five people. But a rare compliance event can still matter a great deal. Record the rationale rather than forcing every impact into a seat count.

### Directional ratings need reasons
High, medium, and low ratings are useful in a workshop, but each needs a short rationale and an evidence status. "High, because about 200 agents do this several times a day (champion's estimate, to confirm with the ticketing data)" is defensible. "High" on its own is not.

### Avoid three common errors
@src S13
- **Treating addressable users as active users.** Not everyone who could use a capability will.
- **Double-counting.** The same saved minutes can't be claimed twice under two benefit lines.
- **Monetizing too early.** Use financial modeling only after the assumptions are explicit and labeled.

### Combine value drivers, carefully
@src S66
@quote S66 | Most use cases deliver multiple value drivers
Glean's value-selling training notes that most use cases deliver multiple value drivers. A complete customer view, for example, can improve both growth and efficiency. Mapping those connections makes a stronger narrative and speaks to several stakeholders at once. It also raises the risk of double counting, so state which driver each number belongs to.

Figure: stack | The parts of impact | Three stacked parts of impact: reach, the number of people affected; frequency, how often the work happens; and consequence, what each occurrence costs in time, quality, or risk.
- Reach :: How many people the work affects
- Frequency :: How often it happens
- *Consequence :: What each occurrence costs in time, quality, or risk

### Practice
Compare two use cases: a broad, low-friction policy lookup used by most employees, and a rare, high-risk approval process in finance.

### Worked response
- **Policy lookup:** record the population and frequency, such as "most employees, a few times a month" (to confirm), and the time per lookup. Impact is mainly capacity and experience.
- **Approval process:** record its severity and controls, such as what an error costs and which controls apply. Impact is mainly risk, and seat count understates it.

Discuss value and feasibility separately for each. Leave unsupported monetary claims unquantified until the inputs are confirmed.

## SBP-106-L04 Effort and technical gotchas
@src S05 S23

Effort is where optimistic use cases fail. A simple-sounding request can depend on several systems, complex permissions, or actions that require security review. Surface these risks in discovery, not after a demo.

### What drives effort
@src S05
@quote S05 | write actions into critical systems trigger a whole new review path and delay.
@quote S05 | stakeholder expects perfect joins and aggregations from a chat prompt when the use case needs deterministic SQL/BI.
Glean's partner enablement lists technical gotchas to surface in discovery:

Table: Technical gotchas
| Area | What to watch for | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Data and connectors | The source of truth is unclear; no native connector; immature or rate-limited APIs; on-premises systems | "Where is the canonical record, and which system wins on conflicts?" |
| Data footprint | A small-sounding use case needs legacy systems, archives, or large files | "How much history does this need?" |
| Permissions and identity | Row- or field-level permissions; multiple directories; shared mailboxes; exceptions | "Who can see what, and are there exceptions?" |
| Structured expectations | Expecting exact joins and aggregations from a chat prompt when the use case needs deterministic SQL or BI | "Is this a lookup or a calculation?" |
| Freshness | Needing real-time results for data indexed periodically | "How current must the answer be?" |
| Actions and write-backs | Assuming the agent can update any system; multi-step transactions; security review of write actions | "What exactly would be written, by whom, with what audit?" |
| Data quality and taxonomy | Inconsistent labels; knowledge only in private chats or local drives | "Where does the real answer live today?" |

### Ask the system owners
Validate feasibility with the people who own the systems. Don't infer it from a use case description. Also consider change management. A technically easy use case still takes effort if it changes how a team works.

> **Boundary:** Don't claim that generic retrieval will perform deterministic joins, or that an agent can write to any system. Verify current capabilities with the Glean account team, and get owner commitments before rating a use case as easy.

### At a glance
Effort hides in the data. Ask system owners about these areas before you rank a use case as low effort.

Figure: cards | Where effort hides | Six cards naming common sources of hidden effort: unclear sources of truth and connectors, permissions and identity, structured data expectations, data freshness, actions and write-backs, and data quality.
- Connectors :: Is there a clear source of truth and a connector?
- Permissions :: Row- or field-level rules; multiple identities
- Structured data :: Exact joins and aggregations expected
- Freshness :: Real-time needs versus periodic indexing
- *Write-backs :: Which systems may the agent update?
- Data quality :: Labels, ownership, and tribal knowledge

### Practice
A use case calls for an agent that reads five systems and updates a CRM record after each customer call. Identify at least four effort risks.

### Worked response
- **Connector availability** for all five systems, and whether each source is canonical.
- **Identity and permissions:** whether users see only the records they're entitled to.
- **Data consistency** across systems, such as conflicting account names or fields.
- **An approved write-action model:** what is written, and the audit trail, idempotency, and handling of partial failures.
- **Security review** of write actions into the CRM.

Mark each as unresolved and assign a validation owner before rating effort.

## SBP-106-L05 Run the impact-versus-effort matrix
@src S05 S02

The 2x2 matrix makes prioritization visible and fast. It is a tool for directional agreement, not a calculator.

### The four quadrants
@src S05
@quote S05 | Score gut-feel H/M/L, place use cases, then circle 2–3 quick wins and 1–2 strategic bets.
Table: Impact vs. effort quadrants
| Quadrant | Impact | Effort | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quick wins | High | Low | Prime candidates for the first wave |
| Strategic bets | High | High | Select one or two for deeper discovery and phasing |
| Nice-to-haves | Low | Low | Keep for later; don't let them crowd out quick wins |
| Deprioritize | Low | High | Park, with reasons |

### Five steps, live with the customer
@src S05
1. **Review the list** in the shared document.
2. **Score quickly:** high, medium, or low for impact and effort.
3. **Place on the grid** as text boxes or sticky notes.
4. **Highlight picks:** two or three quick wins and one or two bets, where appropriate. These counts are guidance, not a rule.
5. **Validate with the sponsor:** "Does this reflect where to start?"

Keep each use case's evidence and rationale next to the visual.

### The matrix is not ROI
A matrix is not a numerical ROI calculation. New evidence can and should move a use case. Never hide a connector blocker to keep a favorite in the quick-win quadrant.

### At a glance
Score live with the customer, then validate the picks with the sponsor. The matrix ranks options; it is not an ROI model.

Figure: flow | Running the matrix live | Five steps for running the impact/effort matrix with the customer: review the list, score quickly, place each item on the grid, highlight the picks, and validate them with the sponsor.
- Review the list :: Confirm each row is specific
- Score quickly :: Impact and effort, with reasons
- Place on the grid :: Make disagreements visible
- Highlight picks :: A small first wave
- *Validate with the sponsor :: Agreement, not assumption

### Practice
A workflow has very high reach, but it depends on a custom connector that nobody has verified. Where do you place it?

### Worked response
Not in quick wins. Treat it as a strategic bet, or mark its effort as unresolved. Assign feasibility discovery for the connector. Consider a narrower first wave that uses sources already available, for example the same workflow limited to content in reachable systems, while the connector question is answered.

## SBP-106-L06 Finalize first-wave scope and evidence
@src S05 S13 S26

The first wave is the set of use cases that go into Day-in-the-Life interviews and, later, the executive case. Each needs enough detail to be owned, measured, and tested.

### What each first-wave use case needs
@src S05 S13
@quote S13 | Can the customer provide baseline data? Do they track the KPIs we'd want to measure?
- **A business owner and a technical owner.**
- **A metric and baseline plan:** what will be measured, where, and over what period.
- **Dependencies:** connectors, permissions, content quality, and actions.
- **Uncertainties:** what is still assumed.
- **DITL participants and dates.**
- **The next milestone.**

### When to use richer scoring
@src S13
A simple matrix is usually enough for live alignment. Glean's Value Playbook also provides an eight-dimension scoring model that covers factors such as business value potential, measurability, time to value, and cross-functional applicability. This can help expose differences between close candidates.

> **Caution:** The playbook's scoring model rates some complexity dimensions so that a higher score is better. Interpret those dimensions carefully before adding scores together. Don't turn the raw total into an automated feasibility gate. This course uses explicit rationale and human review.

### At a glance
A first-wave use case is ready for DITL when every field below is filled in or explicitly marked unknown.

Figure: cards | First-wave readiness | Six cards listing what each first-wave use case needs: business and technical owners, a metric and baseline plan, dependencies, uncertainties, DITL participants and dates, and the next milestone.
- Owners :: A business owner and a technical owner
- Metric and baseline :: What will be measured, and how
- Dependencies :: Data, connectors, and access
- Uncertainties :: Named, each with an owner
- DITL participants :: People and dates confirmed
- *Next milestone :: The decision this work feeds

### Practice
Write a first-wave row for "approved support guidance lookup" in the fictional support scenario.

### Worked response
- **Business owner:** support operations director. **Technical owner:** knowledge-base and platform owner.
- **Data:** the approved knowledge base and past case resolutions. Assumption: agents' permissions mirror the knowledge-base groups, to be confirmed.
- **Metric plan:** handle time for an agreed case class from the ticketing system, plus a resolution-quality guardrail and an agreed baseline window.
- **DITL:** two tier 1 agents and one team lead, with dates booked.
- **Risks:** article currency and ownership; content that lives only in team chat.
