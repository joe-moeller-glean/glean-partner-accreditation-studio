# SBP-108 Business Value and ROI

## SBP-108-L01 Four-part business value architecture
@src S13

Every Glean value case, from a light advisory note to a full executive business case, has the same structure. Only the depth changes.

### The four parts
@src S13
@quote S13 | Business cases connect four things: problem statement, justification, ROI, and plan.
@quote S13 | Every business case must answer: what problem, why Glean, how much impact, and how we'll prove it.
Table: The four-part value architecture
| Part | Question it answers | What goes in it |
|---|---|---|
| Problem statement | What problem, and why now? | The business problem in the customer's language, the trigger for urgency, and who owns it |
| Justification | Why Glean, and why is it credible? | The mapped workflow change, the data and feasibility evidence, and the differentiators that matter here |
| ROI | How much impact? | The quantified benefit range, the costs, the assumptions, and the evidence labels |
| Plan | How will we prove and realize it? | Success criteria, baselines, instrumentation, customer commitments, timeline, and handoff |

### Use the customer's language
@src S13
@quote S13 | Not "they need better search" — that's our language.
Glean's Value Playbook makes the point sharply: "they need better search" is Glean's language. The customer's language sounds like "our engineers spend two hours per incident searching for runbooks." Write the problem statement the way the business owner would say it.

### Hard, soft, and directional value
@src S13
@quote S13 | Directional value — hypothesis-grade, backed by benchmarks rather than customer data
The ROI section must keep three kinds of value distinct throughout:
- **Hard-dollar:** directly hits the P&L, such as cancelled tool licenses, avoided hires, or measurable ticket deflection.
- **Soft-dollar:** real but indirect, such as time saved, faster ramp, or improved satisfaction.
- **Directional:** hypothesis-grade, backed by benchmarks rather than customer data.

> **Boundary:** A savings number without an implementation and measurement plan is incomplete. Never mix hard-dollar, soft-dollar, and directional benefits in one headline total.

### Combine growth and cost reduction
@src S66
@quote S66 | Cost reduction is easier to quantify
Glean's value-selling training makes a practical point about the mix of value in a business case. Cost reduction is easier to quantify, so it often anchors the hard-dollar case. Growth drivers tend to have stronger executive impact. The training's conclusion is that powerful business cases combine both growth and reduction elements, each labeled for how firmly it can be measured.

Figure: compare | Hard, soft, and directional value | A comparison of three kinds of value with how each is treated: hard-dollar value, which changes a budget line and can be counted; soft-dollar value, which creates capacity that must be redeployed to count; and directional value, which is real but not yet monetized.
- Kind of value || How to treat it
- Hard-dollar || Changes a budget line; count it with evidence
- Soft-dollar || Capacity created; count only if redeployed
- *Directional || Real but unmonetized; describe, don't add up

### Practice
Sort four items into the four parts: a workflow map, a risk-adjusted benefit range, a baseline plan, and a strategic priority from the CFO.

### Worked response
- **Strategic priority:** problem statement.
- **Workflow map, plus feasibility evidence:** justification.
- **Risk-adjusted benefit range, with costs:** ROI.
- **Baseline plan, with owner, timeline, and reallocation activity:** plan.

## SBP-108-L02 Translate time to business impact
@src S13 S19

Saved time is where most value cases start, but it isn't where they should end. Executives fund outcomes, so the case must explain what happens with the time.

### Four translation paths
@src S13
@quote S13 | Hours saved don't appear on the P&L. What the customer does with those hours does.
Glean's Value Playbook describes four paths from productivity to business impact:
1. **Capacity created → business metric impact.** Freed time is redeployed to more cases, more deals, or higher-value work.
2. **Cost avoidance.** Tickets deflected, tools consolidated, hires avoided.
3. **Revenue acceleration.** Faster deal preparation, higher win rates, shorter sales cycles.
4. **Risk reduction.** Faster incident resolution, better compliance, fewer errors.

For each path, ask what the customer will actually do with the saved time and which system-of-record metric should change. A benefit mechanism needs an owner, not just a multiplier.

### Classify carefully
- Saved support effort may let a team handle more cases. If the customer confirms an avoided hire, that becomes hard-dollar cost avoidance, with evidence.
- Faster preparation might create selling capacity. Claiming revenue requires much stronger, customer-specific attribution.

> **Boundary:** Don't label all salary-equivalent time as cash savings, assume layoffs, or claim capacity value and a fully overlapping business benefit for the same minutes. State your exclusions to prevent double counting.

### Ask who cares
@src S71
Glean's business-case training uses one test for every translation: who cares? An individual cares about saved minutes. A CFO cares about revenue, cost, and risk. Each path below moves a time estimate into terms a budget holder recognizes, and each needs a named owner who confirms the link.

Figure: cards | Four translation paths | Four cards showing how capacity can translate into business impact: capacity redeployed to a business metric, cost avoided, revenue accelerated, and risk reduced. Each needs an owner who confirms the link.
- *Capacity to a business metric :: Redeployed time moves a KPI
- Cost avoidance :: Hiring, contractors, or spend avoided
- Revenue acceleration :: Faster cycles or higher conversion
- Risk reduction :: Fewer errors, exposures, or incidents

### Practice
Classify three benefits: 500 saved analyst hours a year, a cancelled contract for an overlapping tool, and an estimated increase in win rate.

### Worked response
- **Saved analyst hours:** capacity, or soft-dollar value, until a specific action, such as absorbing growth without a new hire, realizes it.
- **Cancelled tool contract:** hard-dollar, supported by the contract value and the cancellation evidence.
- **Win-rate increase:** directional, unless a credible customer-specific measurement links the change to Glean.

## SBP-108-L03 Evidence quality and measurement design
@src S13 S34

The strength of a value claim depends on its evidence. Label the evidence honestly and design the measurement before you need it.

### Three evidence layers
@src S13
@quote S13 | Value should be triangulated across three evidence layers. Platform telemetry, user voice, and system-of-record outcomes. Any single layer can mislead. All three converging is proof.
Table: Evidence layers
| Layer | Examples | What it can show |
|---|---|---|
| Platform telemetry | Usage, active users, queries, agent runs | Adoption and engagement, not revenue |
| User voice | Interviews, surveys, observations | Perceived benefit and qualitative insight; directional |
| System-of-record outcomes | Ticketing, CRM, HR, finance data | Operational and financial change |

Any single layer can mislead. Glean's Value Playbook treats self-report as directional at best, and says to corroborate it with telemetry or system-of-record data. Survey data alone is never sufficient proof.

### Design the measurement
For every metric, define:
- **Baseline:** the "before," with its period.
- **Target:** the hypothesis for the "after."
- **Data origin:** the system of record.
- **Period:** a comparable window before and after.
- **Owner:** the person who supplies and validates the data.

> **Note:** Evidence-strength terminology varies across Glean materials. Describe the evidence explicitly ("ten self-reported survey responses, no baseline") rather than relying on a label alone. Don't publish internal benchmark figures or weights unless they have been cleared.

### Use the customer's own data
@src S73
@quote S73 | Present multiple ROI scenarios and use company-specific surveys to anchor calculations
Glean's business-case training recommends anchoring calculations in company-specific data, such as a short survey of the people who do the work, rather than generic benchmarks. For time savings, it advises acknowledging that value conversion is imperfect and using conservative recapture rates alongside the survey data. A customer's own numbers are easier for finance to accept and harder to dismiss.

Figure: stack | Three evidence layers | Three stacked evidence layers, strongest at the top: measured data from the customer's systems, customer estimates from interviews or surveys, and assumptions that are labeled and still need validation.
- *Measured :: From the customer's systems of record
- Customer estimate :: From interviews or company surveys
- Assumption :: Labeled; must be validated

### Practice
You have ten user survey responses saying the tool "saves time," with no baseline and no operational data. What claim can you defensibly make?

### Worked response
A directional, self-reported benefit only, for example: "Ten surveyed users report time savings. This is not yet measured." Before presenting any realized financial claim, establish a baseline and corroborate the result with telemetry and system-of-record outcomes, such as handle time in the ticketing system.

## SBP-108-L04 Recapture, adoption and unit-safe calculation
@src S13 S39

A capacity model is a chain of explicit assumptions. Every link needs a unit and an evidence label, and none should be quietly assumed to be 100%.

### The inputs
@src S13
@quote S13 | The productivity recapture rate (ρ) is the most consequential assumption in any Glean value model.
A defensible capacity model accounts for:
- **Population:** addressable users.
- **Adoption:** the share who actively use the capability.
- **Frequency:** events per active user per workday.
- **Time:** minutes per event, converted once to hours.
- **Period:** workdays per year.
- **Removable share and coverage:** how much of the burden Glean can realistically remove, and for what share of events.
- **Recapture:** the share of freed capacity that becomes useful business output.
- **Loaded rate:** the value of an hour, labeled as capacity value, not cash.
- **Uncertainty:** a risk adjustment.

### What recapture means
Glean's Value Playbook calls the productivity recapture rate the most consequential assumption in any value model. It answers this question: of the time Glean saves, how much actually converts into measurable business output? The honest answer is not all of it. Recapture is not the share of users who log in; that is adoption. Ask the business owner for a reallocation plan, meaning what the team will do with the time, before choosing a recapture assumption.

### Avoid double discounting
If the time-saved input is already measured, for example as minutes saved per event, don't apply a removable-share discount to it again. Check what each input means before applying a factor.

> **Boundary:** This module's ROI Lab uses fictional values. Glean's internal calculator defaults are not customer facts. Don't present them as the customer's numbers without validation.

### At a glance
Each factor narrows the estimate. Apply each one once, in a visible formula.

Figure: bars | How the inputs narrow the estimate (fictional) | A bar chart showing a fictional estimate narrowing as each factor is applied: 100 percent of the eligible population's time, 70 percent after adoption, 42 percent after the removable share, and 21 percent after recapture.
- Eligible population time :: 100 :: %
- After adoption :: 70 :: %
- After removable share :: 42 :: %
- *After recapture :: 21 :: %
Caption: Fictional percentages for illustration. Apply each factor once.

### Practice
A colleague's model is "users × minutes saved × hourly rate." Explain why it is incomplete.

### Worked response
It leaves out adoption, event frequency, and the time period. It doesn't convert minutes to hours explicitly. It ignores how much of the work is really removable, and assumes 100% recapture with no uncertainty adjustment.

Document each input with its evidence label, convert units once, and make sure discounts don't overlap.

## SBP-108-L05 Ranges, costs and sensitivity
@src S13 S39 S40

A single number invites false confidence. Show a range, include the real costs, and use sensitivity to find what to validate next.

### Show three scenarios
Present **conservative**, **base**, and **upside** scenarios with transparent assumptions. In this module's fictional ROI Lab, the base scenario produces a risk-adjusted annual capacity value of $41,472 against a first-year all-in cost of $30,000. That gives a net first-year modeled value of $11,472 and a net ROI of about 38%. The conservative scenario produces $13,608 of risk-adjusted value, which is below cost. Keep it visible.

### Formulas, defined consistently
- **Net ROI** = (benefit − cost) ÷ cost, with benefit and cost over the same period.
- **Benefit/cost multiple** = benefit ÷ cost. This is not net ROI.
- **Simple payback (months)** = cost ÷ (annual benefit ÷ 12). The ROI Lab uses this simplified form, which gives about 8.7 months in the base case. It is not a full cash-flow or NPV model. Say so before comparing it with a multi-year calculator.

Include all the relevant costs: platform, implementation, adoption and enablement, and measurement.

### Use sensitivity to decide what to validate
@src S13
@quote S13 | Every ROI estimate must carry its assumptions, risk adjustments, and evidence tier transparently.
If the range is wide, the assumptions that drive it are where validation effort should go. Sensitivity is not a license to pick the most attractive result.

> **Boundary:** Don't compare an annual benefit with a three-year cost, and don't call a negative-return scenario a success. Preserve the downside result and explain its drivers.

### Show more than one scenario
@src S73
Glean's business-case training recommends presenting multiple ROI scenarios rather than a single number. Showing the conservative case alongside base and upside lets a finance reviewer see how the outcome depends on adoption and recapture, and which assumptions matter most.

Figure: bars | Three scenarios (fictional) | A bar chart of three fictional annual benefit scenarios: conservative at 1.2 million dollars, base at 2.0 million, and upside at 3.1 million. The conservative case is emphasized because it shows whether the decision holds under cautious assumptions.
- *Conservative :: 1.2 :: $M
- Base :: 2.0 :: $M
- Upside :: 3.1 :: $M
Caption: Fictional values for illustration only.

### Practice
The modeled ROI ranges from negative to strongly positive across the three scenarios. Name the first assumptions you would validate.

### Worked response
Validate the time burden per event, the active population and adoption, the event frequency, and the recapture rate, with a reallocation plan. Then confirm scope, cost, and the quality guardrails.

State clearly whether the modeled benefits are capacity or cash. Keep the downside result in the readout, with an explanation of what would have to be true for each scenario.

## SBP-108-L06 Operational metrics and value handoff
@src S13 S34 S28

Value promised in pre-sales has to be provable after go-live. Metric cards make that possible, and they carry the value case into the success plan.

### The metric card
@src S13
@quote S13 | Pre-sales value is only credible if it creates a measurable path for post-sales proof.
Table: Metric card (fictional support example)
| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Definition | Mean active handle time for password and access cases |
| Unit and cohort | Minutes per case; tier 1 agents in the pilot region |
| Baseline | Four-week sample before rollout (owner: support operations) |
| Target | A hypothesis, set after the baseline is reviewed |
| System of record | Ticketing system timestamps |
| Data owner | Support operations analyst |
| Collection timing | Before go-live, then at 30, 60, and 90 days |
| Quality guardrail | First-contact resolution rate and re-open rate |
| Caveats | Seasonality; case-mix changes; other process changes |

### Carry it forward
@src S13
Glean's Value Playbook says pre-sales value is only credible if it creates a measurable path for post-sales proof. The plan should cover success criteria, baselines, instrumentation, customer commitments, the timeline, and the handoff to post-sales teams. Adoption metrics help explain why value is or isn't appearing, but they don't replace the business outcome.

> **Boundary:** Never use an aspirational target as a baseline. If the data isn't available, record the gap and assign measurement work. Never fabricate a before-and-after comparison.

### At a glance
The metric cards you write in the Blueprint should become the measures tracked after go-live. If they're vague, nobody can show at renewal whether the investment worked.

Figure: flow | The metric card's lifecycle | A four-step lifecycle for a metric card: defined in DITL, used in the executive business case, measured during validation, and reviewed after go-live.
- Defined in DITL :: Baseline, owner, and data origin
- Used in the business case :: Ranges and assumptions
- Measured in validation :: Against agreed criteria
- *Reviewed after go-live :: Shows value at renewal

### Practice
Complete the baseline field of a metric card when there is no historical timing data at all.

### Worked response
**Baseline:** not yet measured. **Owner:** support operations. **Action:** time a sample of the agreed case class for four weeks before rollout, or enable ticket timestamps if they aren't captured.

The target stays a hypothesis until the baseline has been reviewed. Track quality guardrails and use comparable cohorts.
